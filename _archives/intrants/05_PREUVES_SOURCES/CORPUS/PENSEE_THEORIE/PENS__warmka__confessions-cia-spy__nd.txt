                                       Preface

It was only upon leaving the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) in 2010 and
entering the private security industry that I learned there was a specific field
called social engineering, also referred to as human hacking. Popular
literature described it as a process by which someone manipulates humans
in order to breach security, whether it be the security of the organization or
the individual’s personal security, and it generally carried a very negative
connotation. Although I was just learning about the approach, I realized that
I had spent much of my CIA career as an intelligence officer who, in fact,
used social engineering skills quite effectively to accomplish my missions.
It became a bit of a personal dilemma for me. Was the intentional
manipulation of fellow human beings something inherently evil? Or was it
simply a tool that could be used for evil as well as good? The more I
thought about the topic, the more the passion stirred within me to analyze
the methodology of social engineering and its impact upon the security of
organizations as well as individuals.
    In 2015, Webster University invited me to teach as an adjunct professor
in their master’s degree program for cybersecurity. While addressing
intelligence and counterintelligence issues as they related to cyberwarfare, I
decided to incorporate my knowledge and skills as a spy in explaining how
individuals are effectively targeted and manipulated by adversaries using a
variety of social engineering techniques.
     What made my course truly impactful for my students was an assigned
capture the flag social engineering project. Modeled somewhat after the
annual Social Engineering Capture the Flag competition held during
computer security conferences in Las Vegas, I assigned each of my students
a U.S. corporate entity as their social engineering target. The purpose of the
exercise was to collect as many flags as possible and develop a proposal for
breaching the target entity’s security. The flags were specific pieces of
information that addressed potential exploitable vulnerabilities in a target’s
security system. Each student initially conducted Phase I open-source
intelligence (OSINT) collection on their respective target by scouring a
multitude of sites available on the World Wide Web. Once they
demonstrated proficiency in OSINT collection and exhausted most of the
available resources, I allowed them to move onto Phase II.
     During Phase II, and under careful supervision, my students used
information collected to formulate a variety of social engineering
techniques to use against their target to gather additional flags. They
concluded the project by detailing their Phase I and II processes, what
information they were able to obtain, and possible recommendations for
how they would pursue breaching the company’s security. In addition to
their academic paper, they presented their unique experience in the form of
a presentation before the class. Without exception, students overwhelmingly
completed the project with a much greater awareness regarding the threats
posed by social engineering and how to help protect themselves as well as
their respective organizations from such attacks.
   In addition to my university platform, I began sharing this knowledge by
writing articles for major trade publications as well as speaking at industry
conferences focused on security, fraud and financial systems. While
attendees were typically intrigued by social engineering and wanted to learn
more, I found a severe deficiency in publicly available resources covering
the topic. As a result, the general public continues to remain largely
uninformed and completely susceptible to social engineering attacks
delivered by phishing (email), smishing (SMS), vishing (telephone) or face-
to-face interaction.
    However, what is even more concerning is the general apathy of those
who believe that they will never fall victim to such security breach
attempts. This lack of concern is frequently found at all levels of the
organization, including within the C-suite. Just as the ostrich puts his head
in the sand believing that he will be protected from unseen threats,
corporate leaders also harbor a false sense of security.
   Unfortunately, this apathy and lack of understanding plays perfectly into
the hands of cybercriminals when we consider that according to Verizon’s
2020 Data Breaches Investigations Report (DBIR), more than 90 percent of
successful breaches begin with social engineering. It was for this reason
that I decided to establish the Counterintelligence Institute. My mission is
to assist corporations, nonprofit organizations, academic institutes and city,
state and federal government offices in the protection of their proprietary
information and personal data records against security breach attempts
initiated by social engineering. Through security awareness training, I focus
on transforming the human factor from being the weakest link in security to
becoming the most effective defensive tool against such threats.
      While client organizations and their employees benefit from these
training programs, my intention with writing this book is to be able to help
all individuals understand these threats and to improve their security
practices both at home and in the workplace. Whether you are a CEO of a
major corporation, a dentist, a teacher, a homemaker or retiree, this
information is relevant to you.
        To help provide a greater understanding of social engineering
methodology, I showcase numerous examples throughout this book. While
some of the stories may be fictionalized, the methodologies described
throughout all these examples are very real.
     As a former spy, I know that deception and manipulation can be very
powerful forces. Denying that they exist will only increase their power over
you.

   Sao Paulo, Brazil
    16 April 2017




It had been two months since his last visit. Jean Claude checked into his
usual hotel in Sao Paulo and reviewed the brief he had received two days
ago. While the requirement was standard—penetrate a target entity and
steal vital intelligence—each mission offered unique challenges, along with
an assortment of associated risks.
      His career as an intelligence operative had started in 1985 when he
joined France’s Directorate-General for External Security (DGSE), where
he was trained to target both national governments and private companies.
He was proud to serve his country by securing intelligence for the French
government and, by extension, French multinational organizations that
hungered for information that allowed them to always maintain a
competitive edge. Since his retirement nearly five years earlier, Jean Claude
had steady work as a freelancer in the world of industrial espionage—his
expertise was in high demand.
      This most recent client was interested in securing the program for
XENOX 2000, a new software product that Brazilian firm COMTEC was
planning to introduce into the marketplace by early next year. The client
had already obtained some initial intelligence regarding these plans during a
discussion with a software marketing representative at a conference.
     At a very minimum, Jean Claude had to find a way to penetrate
COMTEC and steal a copy of this program. The client had a buyer who
wanted to develop the same product at a much cheaper cost and hopefully
beat COMTEC’s launch of the product to the market. A secondary
requirement was to implant malware that would modify source code in
COMTEC’s program, causing it to fail shortly after its installation on the
end user’s server.
     Jean Claude needed to learn about the organizational structure of
COMTEC to determine who within the company might have direct access
to information regarding product development. His first instinct was to go
online to visit the company’s website, but it did not provide any useful
names or contact information for personnel other than the front office.
     Jean Claude next went to LinkedIn to search under “COMTEC” for
any employees. While no one surfaced who was a current employee, there
was one individual, Luisa Martins, who had previously worked as an
administrative assistant in COMTEC prior to taking an administrative
position with LUMANEX. In addition to these positions, Luisa listed her
academic background and other professional interests. While not currently
at COMTEC, Luisa would likely be able to provide considerable
information regarding her former employer and be less inhibited than if she
currently worked there.
     Using an alias, Jean Claude decided to directly telephone LUMANEX
and ask for Luisa. He identified himself as an HR consultant who found her
profile on LinkedIn and was looking for someone with Luisa’s background.
Would she be willing to have lunch the following day to explore the
potential employment opportunity? Luisa, flattered and full of curiosity,
immediately accepted the invitation.
     Meanwhile, Jean Claude pulled up Google Earth to look at COMTEC
and the immediate vicinity. It appeared to be located directly across the
street from the financial service firm PROVOX. Jean Claude picked up the
telephone, called COMTEC’s main switchboard and asked to speak with the
person responsible for general maintenance. He was immediately
transferred.
     Hello, can I help you?
     Yes, I really need some help. Are you in charge of maintenance?
     Yes, this is Lucas. How can I assist?
      I’m Gerardo from PROVOX across the street, your neighbor. I
shouldn’t say this but the cleaning company we use is not reliable. I want to
see if we can find a better service provider. Are you happy with the company
providing you with this service?
      Yes, indeed. They are very reliable and efficient.
      Wonderful, what’s their name?
      DELTA.
      How often do they come per week?
      Nightly around 8 p.m.
      Maybe we could try them out. What’s the name of the crew chief?
      Everyone refers to him as Bruno. I have not seen him in a while as I’m
usually out of here by 6 p.m.
      I don’t blame you. There is more to life than working! Thanks so much
for your help. I will contact DELTA directly.
      You are so welcome. Good luck.
      Using a new alias, Jean Claude later called COMTEC’s switchboard,
claiming to work for the American-Brazilian Chamber of Commerce. He
advised that they were planning an upcoming cybersecurity event and
wanted to invite the head of COMTEC’s security department as an attendee.
The switchboard operator readily identified Robert Resende as holding this
position. Jean Claude expressed his gratitude for the assistance and stated
that he would forward the invitation by mail.
      Later that evening, Jean Claude passed by the offices of COMTEC at
exactly 8 p.m. and spoke with the night watchman. He requested to speak
with Bruno from DELTA, who came highly recommended to him. Within
ten minutes, Bruno appeared.
      Hello Bruno. My name is Winston Bosquet and I work for Stedman
Security. We are under contract by COMTEC’s head of security, Robert
Resende, to conduct a survey of how COMTEC’s employees properly follow
security practices in the handling of sensitive information. Mr. Resende
does not want anyone in COMTEC to know about his special project, but he
told me that you are someone whom I could completely trust.
      What exactly do you need?
      I would like to sit down with you over coffee to discuss your
observations regarding how COMTEC employees protect their information.
Do they leave things out on top of their desks? Leave computer passwords
on sticky-notes? Leave file cabinet drawers unlocked? Shred sensitive
documentation?
      Bruno readily agreed to discuss these issues over coffee that same
night. Jean Claude paid him R200 ($50) for 20 minutes of his time—and
then asked for one more thing. Could Bruno consolidate and place
COMTEC’s trash in an area where Jean Claude could retrieve it over the
next four days? If so, Jean Claude would pay him R1,000 ($250) upfront for
this assistance. Bruno immediately jumped upon this opportunity for the
additional income.
      Several intact documents were retrieved by Jean Claude over the four
days of collection. One was a complete internal personnel directory
identifying departments, positions, names and contact information. The
other was a draft slideshow presentation regarding the capabilities of the
new software product. Overall, the investment in trash paid dividends.
      Jean Claude then met Luisa for lunch, where he explained his work
recruiting employees for a client who was setting up a new IT firm. Luisa
was one of several people he wanted to meet to see if there might be a good
fit. Over the next hour, Jean Claude poured on his charm by complementing
Luisa on many facets of her personal and professional life. Under the
pretext of potentially wanting to look at COMTEC employees as additional
candidates, Jean Claude was able to acquire information on key
personalities there.
      Obtaining the personnel directory in advance of this meeting was very
beneficial. Of the 10 individuals who worked in the programming
department, one appeared to be a very attractive target. His name was
Sunjay Kumar, and Luisa described him as an Indian national who arrived
in Sao Paulo two years ago. She depicted Sunjay as very well-mannered
and a hard worker, but he missed his family back home and was having a
hard time adjusting to life in Brazil. Most importantly, he was not very
happy at COMTEC. He did not have a very good relationship with his
department head. Luisa commented that Sunjay confided to her several
months ago that he hoped to find other employment in Brazil or return back
to India. For Jean Claude, this was very valuable insight. He concluded the
meeting by telling Luisa that he would get back in contact when it was time
to hire, and requested that she keep their conversation strictly confidential.
      The next day Jean Claude telephoned COMTEC asking for Sunjay
Kumar. Without going into details, Jean Claude said he was with the firm
AVANTE and wanted to meet for dinner regarding a potential employment
opportunity—Sunjay came highly recommended by a former employee of
COMTEC. Although a bit hesitant, Sunjay agreed to meet Jean Claude for
dinner at the exclusive Intercontinental Hotel, which had been chosen to
impress Sunjay.
     After exchanging pleasantries, Jean Claude explained that he worked
for a consultancy firm located in Quebec, Canada. To alleviate any possible
suspicion, Jean Claude provided Sunjay with a professional business card.
While AVANTE only existed on paper, Jean Claude made sure to backstop
any telephone calls made using a virtual account established through a
business service center. Jean Claude explained to Sunjay that their main
clientele were financial service firms located worldwide. In addition to
assisting with their IT requirements, AVANTE also provided headhunting
services to these clients. Currently, he was recruiting key personnel for a
client that would be expanding their operations into Brazil.
     Sunjay had come to Jean Claude’s attention as a potential candidate,
and he wanted to learn more about Sunjay’s background. If found to be a
potential fit, he would provide Sunjay with more specifics—the
unidentified client did not want it publicly known that they were planning
to enter the marketplace.
     Sunjay spent the next couple of hours detailing his personal and
professional background, including his work experience at COMTEC and
his work on a project called XENOX 2000. While being careful not to
complain about his office environment, Sunjay did mention that he was
looking to advance in his career, whether in Brazil or another country.
     Following the dinner, Jean Claude reached out to a colleague he
wanted to bring in to play the role of the prospective client. Barry, who was
of Indian descent, flew into Sao Paulo for a day of rehearsals with Jean
Claude. After getting Sunjay to sign a nondisclosure agreement, Jean
Claude introduced “Pankaj” to Sunjay as head of the overseas expansion
project for Indian IT giant PTU DATA. Fully familiar with Indian customs
and speaking in Sunjay’s native Punjabi, Pankaj was able to quickly
establish rapport with Sunjay. Pankaj laid out the ostensible plan for
opening offices in Brazil and stated that Sunjay was one of three people he
was considering for the role of programming manager. In such a capacity,
Sunjay would oversee a staff of 15 people and expect to earn twice his
current salary, in addition to annual bonuses.
      Sunjay sat on the edge of his seat—this position sounded like a chance
of a lifetime. He wondered to himself whether he would be chosen over the
other two candidates, but Pankaj added that Sunjay was a strong preference
given his Indian background that would facilitate his ability to work with
more senior PTU DATA managers. While everything looked good on paper,
Pankaj needed to see an example of Sunjay’s best programming work.
Sunjay would need to provide a copy of the software program he was
currently working on. If Sunjay was not ultimately offered the position, he
would still be paid R50,000 ($12,500) for the program.
      Sunjay justified his decision to assist Pankaj for several reasons. He
saw this as a golden opportunity to move up in his career and felt personal
satisfaction in helping a company from his homeland. He wanted to get
back at his boss, who did not appreciate his hard work and potential, and
the R50,000 would allow him to pay off some debts.
      Sunjay used a special storage media device Pankaj had passed to him
to download the program from COMTEC, but it also surreptitiously
uploaded the malware with the altered code. Jean Claude got the USB from
Barry and provided the program to his client, for which he was handsomely
rewarded. After numerous customer complaints and significant bad press
regarding XENOX 2000 and technical failures, another firm launched a
very similar product and took the market by storm. Sunjay, who received
his R50,000, was never contacted again.

As a child, I was raised in a very strict Catholic family. My parents made
every effort to ensure that my five siblings and I were properly educated in
the Catholic faith and dutifully practiced teachings focused on honesty,
loyalty and philanthropy in our daily lives. At the age of 14, I left home to
enter St. Lawrence Seminary, which prepares young men for possible
vocations into the priesthood. My four years at the central Wisconsin
seminary provided great opportunity for personal growth and reflection, and
I was most impacted by summer mission trips to the east coast of
Nicaragua. Even though I eventually decided to leave seminary studies,
these mission experiences left me with a strong desire to someday work in
Latin America.
     Several years later, after obtaining my master’s degree in international
business management, I landed a position with a South Florida bank with
operations throughout Latin America. My focus was on private banking,
managing the accounts of high net-worth clients from various South
American countries including Argentina, Brazil, Chile and Colombia.
     During my time in this role, I learned that a successful private banker
is one who excels at maintaining close and trusting relationships with
clients. If the clients like you, your network will easily grow through
referrals provided to their family members, friends and business associates.
After establishing a roster of close working relationships, I moved on from
private banking and began to work as a personal investment and security
advisor to several clients.
      Due to my frequent travel to Latin America, my network of contacts
and fluency in Spanish and Portuguese, I was eventually approached by the
Central Intelligence Agency (CIA). While I had never contemplated
working for the U.S. government, let alone the CIA, I was intrigued and
willing to explore the opportunity. They wanted me to become a case
officer, collecting foreign intelligence information to be used by U.S.
officials to make better informed policy decisions. This information was
non-public, sensitive and invaluable. The CIA would provide me with the
intelligence requirement and identify who within an organization would
likely have access to it. My job would be to gain access to such a person
and eventually manipulate them into providing this information to me. In
essence, I would learn how to creatively breach the security of a target
organization to steal their most precious secrets. Such organizations would
include various government ministries, senior government leadership
circles and strategic state infrastructure. After several weeks of reflection, I
made the decision to join the CIA.
      You may think it peculiar for someone who seriously considered
entering the priesthood to instead take a job with the underlining
requirement to lie and steal. Truthfully, it was an initial moral conundrum
for me. However, I was eventually able to rationalize it—I believed that this
approach was the only way to successfully complete the mission, an
undertaking that would be of ultimate service to my country.
      During my career with the CIA, I learned the intricacies of befriending
strangers, manipulating people through electronic communication and
meticulously studying human behavioral patterns. The approaches I used—
which I later understood to be social engineering—are practiced by all
kinds of people, from the eager salesperson to the nation-state hacker.
      I bring up my personal story to emphasize the variety of threat actors
behind today’s security breaches—who they are, what they are after and
what drives them. Threat actors can be broken down into the following
categories: state actors, organized criminal groups, industrial competitors,
activist groups and malicious insiders.
    State Actors
    Practically every nation state in the world has an intelligence apparatus
that strategically collects intelligence for both defensive and offensive
purposes. The most active nations in information gathering include China,
Great Britain, France, Iran, Israel, North Korea, Russia and the United
States.
      While the U.S. intelligence community does not typically collect
information on commercial companies overseas, many foreign intelligence
services do target U.S. corporations. They are trying to steal technology by
acquiring intellectual property. Advancements in technology typically
require years of research and significant development costs. If they can
effectively steal such technology, they can save considerable time and
money while propelling their respective industries to better compete with
U.S. companies. China is the most notorious for this.
      Another area of focus by some state actors in conducting data breaches
is gaining access to a target entity’s customer or personnel data. Just as
large companies such as Facebook and Google build databases on
consumers to be used for marketing purposes, some state actors appear to
be gathering detailed data on U.S. citizens for potential exploitation in
future intelligence operations.
      Many Americans received their personal wakeup call when they
learned about the security breaches suffered in 2014 and 2015 by the Office
of Personnel Management (OPM). OPM is the repository of personal
information collected and analyzed by the U.S. government prior to issuing
an individual with a security clearance. There is no single database in the
world that has more information on the millions of U.S. citizens it contains.
Approximately 21.5 million current and former federal employees received
a letter from OPM advising that their personal information was breached,
but all they were offered in return was a free service to monitor their online
identity and credit reports.
      A second notable security breach occurred at the credit bureau Equifax
in 2017. Here, the hackers were able to gain access to the sensitive credit
information of nearly 150 million Americans. Contrary to many smaller
breaches where consumer credit card account information is sold through
the underground market on the dark web and then used for identity theft or
to gain access to other websites, the information acquired in the Equifax
breach, as of this writing, has not surfaced. Cybersecurity experts believe
that this information was not stolen for criminal activity, but rather by a
foreign nation state for intelligence purposes.
      According to U.S. intelligence experts, China was behind both
breaches, which calls into question their objective. Was it to steal
information to conduct identity theft, or is it for something much, much
bigger? Think about the value of this information to a state actor such as the
Chinese. The OPM data specifically relates to individuals who have access
to U.S. intelligence secrets. Such information provides an intelligence
service invaluable insight into motivations and vulnerabilities of these
individuals that could be leveraged when trying to recruit them as potential
intelligence sources. While the information stolen from Equifax is not as
broad as that taken from OPM, it does identify individuals who are
experiencing financial difficulties. Combining data from OPM along with
Equifax would be extremely valuable to such an intelligence service.
      Beyond OPM and Equifax, I would be remiss not to highlight that the
industry with the greatest number of ongoing security breaches is
healthcare. If your healthcare provider or insurer was one of the thousands
that have suffered breaches over the past several years, what data was
potentially collected on you by the perpetrator of the crime? Your
biographic history, your health history, employment details, social security
number or credit card numbers could have been compromised. How could
this information be used by someone with nefarious intent? While there is
no evidence that China is behind the breaches in the healthcare industry,
such information is dangerously powerful in the hands of any intelligence
service.
      Regardless of the ultimate collection goals sought by state actors when
breaching security and stealing information, their intelligence officers are
predominantly motivated by extreme loyalty to their country—it is seldom
about money. Just like U.S. intelligence operatives, state actors risk
discovery and potential incarceration in a foreign land.
     Organized Crime
     The vast majority of security breaches, including cyberattacks, can be
attributed to criminal groups. Whether it’s a small band of criminals or part
of a large criminal syndicate, the motivation is predominantly financial
gain. Think of them like art thieves—they generally do not steal works of
art for their own collections but instead look to sell them on the black
market. The biggest market for stolen digital information on the internet is
the dark web.
      Visualize the internet as an iceberg. At the very top, above the water’s
surface, is an area seen and accessed by Google and other search engines.
This layer, commonly referred to as the surface web, represents just 5
percent of the entire internet. The other 95 percent beneath the surface is
referred to as the deep web and contains locations that are not indexed and
therefore not accessible by standard search engines. Within this deep web
exists markets where criminals buy and sell information, as well as other
products and services, while remaining anonymous and utilizing digital
currencies.
      While a significant portion of breaches may indeed be conducted by
criminal elements, it is difficult to separate these attacks from those planned
or orchestrated by nation states. In some cases, nation states will use
organized criminal groups as a force multiplier in undertaking such actions
while also deflecting attribution away from the nation state. Additionally,
organized crime may approach nation states as potential clients for stolen
data.
      Beyond stealing data for internal use or resale, criminals will also
target entities through other tactics. One of the most popular criminal
schemes is ransomware, where the criminal breaches security to encrypt the
target’s data and hold it hostage. In return for the payment of the ransom,
usually in the form of bitcoin, the criminal will provide the victim with a
decryption code. Once infected with ransomware, victims have few options.
Unless they have previously installed a well-isolated data backup system
with regular updates, the loss of access to their data practically paralyzes
their operational activities, which can quickly result in substantial revenue
loss. While government authorities typically recommend against paying the
ransom, such a payment is usually much cheaper than suffering a shutdown
of their business until their IT system and database can be rebuilt. For this
reason, most victims end up paying the ransom. No organization or
individual is safe from such attacks. They have hit small to large businesses,
academic institutions and municipal, state and federal government offices as
well as private individuals. According to a report by cybersecurity firm
Emisoft, ransomware attacks cost the U.S. economy over $7.5 billion in
2019 and are predicted to only increase in the coming years.
      Another increasingly popular tactic used by criminal groups in security
breaches are business email compromise (BEC) schemes. This approach
usually targets companies working with foreign suppliers as well as
companies that regularly conduct wire transfers. In such cases, criminals are
able to insert themselves in email correspondence between managers and
those who might conduct wire transfers based upon their instructions.
Posing as a manager, the criminal will send a well-crafted email providing
urgent instructions to send a wire transfer to what appears to be a normal
recipient. However, the specific wire transfer instructions are to a fraudulent
bank account. Once the funds are received, the criminal quickly withdraws
these funds before discovery. According to the U.S. Treasury, BEC attacks
in 2018 were costing U.S. companies over $300 million per month.
    Corporate Espionage
    All corporations continuously collect information on their competitors
to better position themselves within the marketplace. While competitive
intelligence is predominantly collected within legal and ethical constraints,
some companies will cross the line into unethical or illegal collection
methods. At times, they may contract with intelligence operatives to
distance themselves from such activity, failing to realize that discovery
could result in serious criminal charges and a huge financial blowback to
the corporation. More commonly, such firms will use recruitment
techniques to hire new employees who agree to provide proprietary
information they were privy to during their employment with a competitor
firm.
      Regardless of the technique deployed, the unlawful acquisition of
corporate trade secrets is considered industrial espionage.
    Activists
    Activism may include efforts to advocate, block, direct or intervene in
political, social, economic or environmental reform with the goal of
effecting changes in society. This activism can be expressed in numerous
ways and may be either peaceful or violent. Peaceful measures include
writing letters to politicians, placing op-eds in news publications,
conducting public demonstrations or promoting boycotts. Escalated
measures may invoke or promote acts of violence. On the extreme end of
the spectrum are terrorist groups, which typically focus their efforts to
invoke change through acts of terror. While governmental entities and
officials were traditionally the main target for activist causes, more recent
focus has been placed on major corporations. In such cases, activists
attempt to pressure corporations to make changes that may affect the
welfare of employees, the consumer or the environment.
      Activist groups may try to breach the security of an entity to further
their cause through methods such as stealing proprietary information for
public disclosure in order to provide proof of accusations or to embarrass
the entity. Frequently, such breaches end up seriously affecting the financial
wellbeing of the firm. Other breaches may facilitate access into non-public
or restricted spaces of the company for members of activist groups during
planned protests.
    Malicious Insiders
    Threats are not always external to the enterprise—sometimes they
develop from within. These insider threats usually stem from a disloyal
employee of the firm who intends to personally benefit at the expense of the
company. Such individuals are typically disgruntled and sometimes very
angry. They may feel underappreciated, believing that they deserve a higher
position or higher pay, or may feel belittled by their supervisor. In this case,
revenge becomes a motivator, and they want to get back at their company.
Another motivation may be financial. They may be suffering from financial
duress, worried that they will not be able to support their family, or they
may desire to afford certain luxuries in life.
     Ultimately, such insiders will breach security protocols to obtain and
use proprietary information for their own benefit. Some insiders may offer
to sell the information to third parties, such as competitors or vendors trying
to gain contracts with the company. The most frequent way insider threats
come to fruition is when existing employees take proprietary information to
establish their own businesses or to enhance their position with a new
employer.
     A subcategory within the insider threat is a whistleblower, an insider
who decides to disclose sensitive information to government authorities or
to the general public. It usually involves serious financial fraud or a coverup
by management of a process or procedure that is considered detrimental to
consumers. The whistleblower is usually motivated by a desire to correct
what they believe to be a serious wrong within the organization where they
work. Like an activist, they want to effect change.

   Despite the threats outlined above, I frequently encounter individuals
who believe that their organization will not become a target of interest,
especially to foreign intelligence services or organized criminal groups.
They believe their organization has nothing of real value to steal. I then ask
what other entities their organization conducts business with. Many times,
one or more of their clients are high-value targets.
      Criminals and intelligence services will always evaluate whether a
target entity is considered to be hard or soft. The hard target is extremely
difficult to penetrate due to robust security protocols. On the contrary, soft
targets practically leave the door open with a welcome sign posted at the
entrance. When confronted with a hard target, social engineers will identify
and carefully analyze whether entities providing products or services to that
target might provide the perfect conduit for gaining access. Examples of
such service providers for potential exploitation are law firms, CPA firms,
staffing companies, IT management providers and other vendors.
      The Panama Papers leak revealed the amount of sensitive information
that law firms may hold regarding their clients. In this case, more than 2.6
terabytes of data containing 11.5 million files were accessed from a data
breach of the world’s fourth biggest offshore law firm, Panamanian-based
Mossack Fonseca. While the actual identity and methodology used by the
source of the breach remains undisclosed, the information was provided in
August 2016 to the German newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung. The acquired
documentation identified a long list of prominent clients as well as details
regarding methodologies used to hide their assets offshore.
      Numerous national leaders were on the list, including then Pakistani
Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif, ex-interim Prime Minister and former Iraqi
Vice President Ayad Allawi, Ukraine President Petro Poroshenko and
Iceland Prime Minister Sigmundur Davio Gunnlaugsson. Other accounts
were linked to family members or close associates of Egyptian President
Hosni Mubarak, British Prime Minister David Cameron and Russian
President Vladimir Putin.
      While the Panama Papers became a sensationalized breach due to its
size and scope of high-profile victims, it also revealed that most law offices
likely have sensitive client information that would be of value to threat
actors. Such information might reveal details regarding beneficial
ownership of real estate and other assets, merger & acquisition details,
negotiation strategies, debt restructuring, litigation planning and more.
While successful breaches of law offices are on the rise, most law firms
remain complacent towards implementing greater security protocols to
better protect information entrusted to them by their clients. This will
unlikely change until clients begin to conduct due diligence on law firms
and hold them to higher standards.
      Say your firm provides engineering consultancy services, and you
have many clients, both in the private sector as well as with the U.S.
government. You won a bid to provide engineering services for a new state-
of-the-art facility being built for a manufacturer of superconductors. That
entity is a high-priority target for the foreign intelligence service. Instead of
directly trying to penetrate the target entity for collection of proprietary
information, the foreign intelligence service would likely try to penetrate
your firm to obtain the detailed schematics of the facility. They might even
consider recruiting one of your employees with direct unescorted access
into the target facility during the project.
      Two real-life examples from the retail and healthcare industries also
illustrate these risks. In 2013, a security breach at Target compromised
payment accounts of about 41 million customers and the personal details of
approximately 70 million consumers. Numerous lawsuits were filed against
this retailer, and the total costs of the breach exceeded $236 million,
including an initial multi-state settlement of $18.5 million to cover state-
specific costs associated with their investigations of the breach.
Additionally, Target agreed to pay up to $10,000 to consumers who could
prove their data was compromised.
      How was Target penetrated? It turns out that perpetrators of this attack
first breached a third-party HVAC provider based in Pennsylvania. They
then stole the computer gateway credentials of this vendor to gain access to
Target’s network and install malware.
      In the 2018 breach of Managed Health Services (MHS), more than
31,000 plan members had their personal information compromised
including names, insurance ID numbers, addresses, dates of birth, dates of
service and descriptions of medical conditions. While total costs are
undisclosed, this breach took a serious toll on MHS’ reputation within the
health industry.
      How did it happen? While attributable to two separate incidents, the
first breach was associated with a phishing attack at vendor company LCP
Transportation. LCP employees received scam emails, allowing hackers to
access their email accounts. Once the perpetrators penetrated the LCP
network, they were able to find a gateway into MHS.
     Even if you do not believe your organization has anything of
significant value to steal, never assume that you are not a potential target. A
better posture would be asking whether any of the companies you do
business with make attractive targets.

                          Organization

                                                                 —Benjamin Franklin




Mounting an attack against a target or adversary is never conducted in a
void. All available information is collected in advance and carefully
analyzed to come up with the best strategy for maximizing success of the
operation while minimizing any potential compromise of equities. Military
commanders will diligently collect ground truth before sending in their
forces. Terrorists and organized criminal groups looking to kidnap their
target will typically mount surveillance for days or weeks in advance to
gather information regarding that target’s pattern of life. Likewise,
professional social engineers who look to penetrate an organization to steal
data will definitely conduct their research as well.
      As a CIA intelligence officer, I learned to leverage a great internal
resource: analysts who provide targeting packages. They collected
information from various public and nonpublic sources, then conducted an
analysis and helped better guide my targeting operations. However, at
times, I needed to supplement this information with my own research.
      Despite a CIA intelligence officer’s ability to access nonpublic
resources, sometimes the most valuable information is in plain sight.
Thanks to the proliferation of the World Wide Web, there are many public
resources that appear completely harmless to the average person but present
a true vulnerability for a target organization when a social engineer knows
how to exploit it.
    Organization Websites
    My starting point is typically the official website established by the
entity from which I want to acquire information. Out of all available public
resources, this is the medium the entity has complete control over when it
comes to exposing information. While websites back in the late 1990s
typically had only a few pages, today’s organizational websites can exceed
several dozen. They are structured into several categories, each providing
tidbits of information of potential value to social engineers.
      The “About Us” section will usually outline historical information as
well as the entity’s mission statement. These details can provide a window
into the culture of the organization. What do they value, or what do they
want to convey to the public that they value? This can be useful for
structuring a pretext or cover story when I eventually decide to approach a
member of the target organization. This same section may also identify
leadership figures and other key personnel working for the entity. Typically,
it will incorporate their pictures, biography and occasionally even contact
information.
      A “Products and Services” section will detail the commodities the
entity is trying to promote. Once again, this information may be useful
when developing a pretext for initiating contact with a company
representative.
      The “Press Releases/News” portion of a website is somewhat useful
for identifying recent milestones achieved by the entity or its management.
These may include promotions of key personal, contracts obtained, or
strategic acquisitions of companies incorporated into the group. Often, such
press releases will also include brief statements from key officials.
      “Career Opportunities” provide an indication of company growth,
whether geographically or in new products or services. Many times, they
will also include job postings.
      “Contact Us” is a portal through which contact from the outside is
solicited—a welcome opportunity for social engineers.
     Job Postings
     Whether posted on the organization’s website or on one of many job-
posting sites such as Monster, Indeed, CareerBuilder and Glassdoor, job
listings provide a wealth of information. At a bare minimum, listings will
usually reveal the basic preferred IT qualifications sought from an
applicant, which can provide invaluable insight into the software programs
the organization runs on its server.
      An example of this was a job posting recently placed on Indeed by a
midwestern firm looking to hire an AWS Data Architect. The following
details were listed within the job posting under “Technical Requirements.”

             Experience implementing AWS Data Services in production
          Redshift, EMR, EC2, S3, Athena, RDS, DynamoDB, Kinesis,
          Glue, etc.
             Experience developing distributed data platforms using Spark,
          Scala, PySpark, Spark-SQL.
             Experience leveraging Big Data Technologies in Hadoop,
          Hive, HBase, HDFS, Cassandra, Kafka, Sqoop, etc.
             Proficiency in Python, Scala, and/or Java.
             Proficiency in SQL.
             Experience with designing and implementing RDBMS,
          Oracle, PostgreSQL, MySQL, MS SQL Server, etc.

     Prior to the official launch of a new product or service, companies will
frequently hire additional employees to support this growth. The job
descriptions may provide greater insight into the market focus of the target
entity, whether it be growth through current clients, acquisition of new
customer accounts or geographic expansion.
      With a job posting, a company is inviting contact with someone from
the outside, and it provides social engineers an opportunity to submit a
resume to HR. Another approach is to carefully choose someone else within
the company to forward my resume—and potentially attached malware—to
HR. Job postings also provide the ability to interview with the employer,
creating an additional opportunity to gather critical information regarding
the organization. I could even have one of my team members attempt to
obtain employment with the company and become my insider.
    Employer Review Sites
    Glassdoor has a database containing over 8 million company reviews,
leadership and management ratings, salary reports, job interview tips,
employee benefits reports and workplace insights all generated by the
people who know the company best, the employees. Here, social engineers
can gain invaluable insight into what employees like or dislike about
working for the target organization. Reviews discussing great opportunities
for professional growth, awesome management, flexible hours and good
benefits are just as valuable as ratings that reveal a lack of opportunities for
career advancement, antagonistic work environment, incompetent
management, long hours or no benefits.
      These reviews provide the pulse regarding morale within the
organization. Generally, it is much easier to manipulate a disgruntled
employee than someone who is very happy and loyal to their employer.
    Organization Social Media Sites
    While an entity may aggressively use social media to help promote their
products and services, an unintended consequence can be the leakage of
information, which can be invaluable to social engineers. This exposure
results in the creation of vulnerabilities for the organization. Company
employees will often upload photos or videos of themselves in the
workplace to social media sites, providing social engineers with insight into
their physical workspaces.
      When hosting events, some organizations will post floor plans. Might
they be useful to a criminal wanting to make a physical intrusion? Uploaded
pictures will often provide information regarding what actual workspaces
look like. Are there offices that might be more difficult to gain access to, or
are there open cubicle areas? Or perhaps better still, are there shared lounge
areas where employees can just take a seat, resulting in less scrutiny over
who really belongs in the area?
      Gaining an understanding of employee dress code can be useful if I am
sending someone inside and want them to fit in. In addition to dress,
pictures frequently reveal information regarding the types of badges used
by the company and how employees display them. If I know the specific
design of the badge, one can easily be manufactured for flash purposes.
      Occasionally, shared pictures will capture the names of posted WIFI
networks or passwords. Photos taken in front of employee workstations will
frequently provide detailed information regarding the brand and model of
computer hardware. If the screen is left turned on, I can see what browsers
and specific software programs are used within the organization. Sometimes
these photos may even show emails or documents left on the screen.
     Creative Google Searches
     When looking up a topic on Google, most people will only view up to
the first 12 entries. However, there are easily thousands of entries regarding
large companies, and some of the most interesting information for social
engineers is found well beyond the first page of results. For this reason,
conducting creative searches is crucial. A simple search of the company
name and “pdf” may surface employee manuals, employee benefit
packages, IT user guides, executed nondisclosure agreements and other
documents containing sensitive information. The different companies that
are subcontracted by the target company for services such as janitorial, trash
disposal, catering or temporary staff can often be identified within such
documents—a key resource for exploiting contracted service providers to
breach security.
     The Wayback Machine
     A common misconception regarding the internet is that once an
organization has deleted or modified information previously contained on
its corporate website, the original information is no longer available to the
public. Fortunately for social engineers, this is not the case. The Wayback
Machine (www.archive.org/web) is a digital archive of the World Wide
Web. This free resource enables users to see archived versions of web pages
across time, as far back as 1996. Therefore, even if the target organization’s
new security director decided that some of the information on a company’s
website should be removed, the Wayback Machine can be used to
potentially retrieve it.
    Reconnaissance Made Easy
    I do not have to physically walk the street to see what the target’s office
building looks like—I can do it virtually through maps and satellite imagery
offered by Google Maps and Google Earth, which provide images of the
target’s office building and even an individual’s residence. If I wanted to
launch an intrusion into a target office, I would want to learn as much as
possible about access points. For example: Are there guards? Are they
armed? Is there video surveillance? More importantly, I would want to find
out what businesses are near the target location such as coffee shops, bars,
restaurants, fitness centers and office supply stores.
      This type of intelligence is important because it helps me identify
places where individuals from the target facility might hang out during the
workweek, providing opportunities to casually bump into them. That could
result in a conversation where I can carefully elicit information.
Alternatively, it could lead to an opportunity to develop an employee for
use as a future insider source. Other afforded opportunities may be the theft
of the employee’s credential, facilitating an intrusion into the target’s
building or the theft of the employee’s laptop, which likely contains
proprietary information.
      Additionally, I can use geographical information in order to identify
locations in the vicinity that make deliveries to the target’s office, such as
flower shops, restaurants or office supplies. With this information in hand, I
may decide to impersonate someone making a delivery to obtain unescorted
access onto the premises.

    Identification of Employees
    The internet can be exploited to identify the names of many target
organization employees as well as their positions. By far the most
resourceful site used by social engineers for this purpose is LinkedIn—an
estimated 40 percent of a firm’s employees have their own LinkedIn profile.
Other resources for identifying employees include the organization’s
official website and social media pages. The identification of employees is a
crucial step for social engineers in planning their attacks.

Selecting Insiders and Collecting Personal
            Assessment Data
              “A lot of hacking is playing with other people,you know, getting them to do strange
                                               things.”

                                                                               — Steve Wozniak




Gathering     information about organizations is one thing, but selecting,
assessing and manipulating individuals within those organizations takes a
different approach. In general terms, an insider is anyone who has regular
unescorted access into the target organization, whether the physical
premises or the organization’s IT network. This includes all employees
from the receptionist up to the CEO. In addition to employees, contractors
and outsourced services such as security guards, food caterers, cleaning and
trash disposal personnel and IT technicians could become insiders. These
contracted service providers could likely be identified through creative
Google searches.
      Not all potential insiders need to be considered— a small pool of well-
placed individuals on whom I can collect useful assessment data is
sufficient. For example, if an organization has 300 employees, chances are
that they have five to ten potential inside targets worth my consideration.
Learning about their areas of responsibility will help appraise their direct or
indirect access to the information I seek. Upon validation of their likely
access, I will then want to learn as much as possible about them to develop
a personality profile assessment. This assessment, which will include
identified motivations and vulnerabilities, will be key to structuring a plan
to effectively manipulate them.
      Prior to the abundance of resources available on the internet,
intelligence officers initially targeted potential insiders with little
information beyond their name and organizational title. I could only
speculate on their potential access and had no personality profile prior to
approaching them. It was only after a series of face-to-face encounters that I
was able to determine their access and obtain invaluable assessment
information on them. This process would take several months to
accomplish, and in some cases the intended target ultimately did not have
the desired access or lacked personality characteristics that would make
them suitable for manipulation. I would then drop them and move onto
another potential insider—options were very limited.
      Today, the internet provides intelligence officers and criminals alike
with a tremendous amount of resources to accomplish this task with greater
efficiency and total anonymity. While it is commonplace and expected for
organizations to have public information easily searchable online, finding
intimate details about individuals can be more difficult. However, social
media profiles have become key for intelligence gathering on otherwise
private individuals. Hidden in plain sight, the following aspects of popular
social media can provide a surprising amount of information.

    LinkedIn

when targets displayed significant vulnerabilities, such as excessive

these vulnerabilities were hard to control and could lead to serious security
problems.

binge, I lost complete contact with him. He reappeared only moments
before his participation in the next crucial phase of the operation, claiming
to have lost his wallet and cell phone to a group of robbers. While
ultimately a very successful operation, the source’s vulnerabilities posed
security risks to the operation and really tested my patience.

Designing a Pretext for an Approach
         “A con artist’s only weapon is his brain.”

                                                      —Frank W. Abagnale, Catch Me If you can




Once a social engineer has gathered all available useful data on the target
organization and identified plausible insiders, they need to develop the
pretext or cover story for an approach. This pretext needs to be logical as
well as attractive enough to get the insider’s attention. As successful sales
professionals know, it is all about identifying the prospective customer’s
pain and then offering a potential solution to their problem. If the approach
does not appear to benefit them in any way, they typically will not welcome
it.
      Conceptually designing that pretext and then executing the entire plan
was what I most enjoyed during my career with the CIA. It required careful
study of the target, creativity in the formulation of the approach, use of a
variety of interpersonal skills, extreme flexibility and continuous
adaptability. Most of all, such operations energized me and frequently gave
me an adrenaline rush.
      Just as criminals will not introduce themselves as professional
fraudsters to their targets, intelligence officers will not reveal their true
affiliation. As a result, it is crucial that the pretext for the initial approach
has nothing to do with intelligence gathering. Over the course of my career
as an intelligence officer, I assumed multiple identities and portrayed
various professions. Depending upon the circumstances, I could also
pretend to be another nationality than American. This required modifying
my accent in English or the use of three foreign languages I am familiar
with.
      Crucial to the success of a pretext is some degree of cover
backstopping, or creation of the backstory. No matter how strong the
backstopping, successful intelligence officers must fit the profile of the
persona they intend to portray. If they do not, their cover will quickly be
compromised. A common saying during our training was, “If you pretend to
be a duck, you can’t act like a turkey. You have to walk, swim and quack
like a duck.”
      The general public’s perception of working undercover is typically
warped by Hollywood. Films often show law enforcement officers who
infiltrate drug rings or other criminal networks for the purpose of bringing
them down. As part of their cover, the officers flash a lot of cash, wear
expensive clothing and drive sports cars containing an arsenal of weapons
in the trunk. They easily slip back and forth from being undercover for
specific meetings to returning to their normal lives.
      For an intelligence officer, going undercover is no short-term
glamourous adventure. It takes years of training followed by years of
practical experience and seasoning. Initial training focuses on spy tradecraft
—how to conduct clandestine operations without discovery. However,
equally important, if not more so, is the officer’s ability to understand
operational requirements and how to best design and develop their cover to
effectively insert themselves into circles of individuals who can help satisfy
intelligence collection requirements. To be accepted and trusted by such
individuals, intelligence officers cannot display any characteristics that
would arouse the suspicion that they are not who they claim to be.
      An intelligence officer must also adhere to the norms for individuals
that work in the profession they are emulating. In addition to maintaining an
appropriate economic status, they must include a credible legend regarding
the progression of their career and their competency on topics within the
field. The slightest discrepancy can draw suspicion.
      The danger for some officers is the complacent belief that if they have
not been seriously challenged regarding their story, it must be working. In
many cases, people who might suspect that something is strange about the
individual will not directly confront them. However, they will talk amongst
themselves, which could eventually lead to the filing of a report with local
authorities. Once the local jurisdiction has taken an active interest in the
activities of the intelligence officer, the house of cards may quickly come
tumbling down.
      While during my career my activities were never seriously challenged,
I did learn of colleagues working overseas who were compromised. Several
of them were briefly detained by authorities and then expelled from the
country as persona non grata. Others were imprisoned and deprived of their
liberties for extended periods of time. Over the course of the history of the
CIA, there have been many officers who died in service to their country.
Inside the entrance to CIA headquarters is a memorial wall that contains
rows of stars. The inscription above the stars reads, “In honor of those
members of the Central Intelligence Agency who gave their lives in the
service of their country.” As documented in The Book of Honor by Ted
Gup, several of those stars are attributed to undercover intelligence officers
who died in action or while serving life sentences in foreign prisons.
      Intelligence offers are typically required to live their cover 24 hours
per day over extended months, if not years. Perhaps the biggest challenge of
this lifestyle is loneliness. While living in a sea of people, many of whom
could be considered close friends and acquaintances, the officer is never
allowed to be transparent. They will always have to conceal who they really
are. This creates difficulties in developing intimate relationships and can
serve as a true barrier to marriage. Those fortunate enough to establish a
family will also place the burden of secrecy upon their loved ones. Many
times, the stress of the job and managing the web of lies can literally tear
couples and families apart.
      The Russian Illegals Program is a prime example of how extreme the
development of cover can be. Run by the Russian Foreign Intelligence
Service (known by its Russian abbreviation, SVR), candidates are selected
and isolated at a very young age for preparation to become immersed into a
specific foreign country and assume the identity of a citizen of that chosen
country. They receive extensive training regarding the history, culture and
traditions of their future “homeland,” as well as subtleties in the local
language. While these illegals have been known to operate in several
countries, the most notable recent example came to light in the United
States with the June 27, 2010 arrest by the FBI of ten Russian illegal agents.
Among them was Ana Chapman, who gained the most attention from the
press due to her femme fatal attributes. Shortly after the arrests, the agents
were part of a prisoner exchange between Russia and the U.S.
      Probably one of the best Hollywood-generated depictions of illegal
agents is the series The Americans. While this six-season drama accurately
reflects many of the challenges faced by illegals, the portrayed operational
tempo during a 24- to 48-hour period far exceeds what most intelligence
officers would face over the course of several weeks, if not months.

    In reality, most threat actors will not invest in the time and effort to
develop the same depth of cover sought by true intelligence professionals.
However, there are still a number of factors they will need to consider when
designing their strategy to ensure that their cover pretext will assist them in
gaining access to their target while defending against any cursory due
diligence resulting from curiosity or suspicion.
      This process can be illustrated by a criminal group’s interest in
breaching the security of a large South Florida wealth management
company, Petra Wealth Group (PWG). Let’s say the criminal enterprise
seeks to uncover the identities of PWG’s top high net worth clients. In
addition to their names, the criminals would likely seek additional sensitive
information including their personal family details, business holdings,
financial transactions and account information. Such information could be
sold on the dark web or to directly target one or more clients.
      The criminal group would first attempt to identify who within PWG
might have access to such information. While their initial internal
discussions focused on targeting someone within PWG’s IT department,
they soon realized that such an insider would likely not have access to
anything more than transactional data such as account numbers, account
balances and wire transfers. This approach would not surface the names of
individuals who were the account holders, nor any specific information
regarding their personal lives or business dealings. It was finally concluded
that such details are only known by PWG’s account managers, who
maintain a separate file on each client detailing the history of the account.
While specific individuals may be assigned to maintain the relationship
with their own clients, such information must remain accessible to other
account managers in the event they need to service the customer in the
absence of the assigned account manager. Furthermore, these account
managers will also have access to many of the same transactional reports
that are automatically generated by the IT department.
      Now that the category of target insider has been identified, the criminal
group will attempt to identify several candidates. They learn that PWG has
six full-time account managers. Out of these six, the group has been able to
obtain substantial information on four of them through the review of their
social media profiles, along with other internet research.
      They identify Javier Acosta as a very attractive candidate. He joined
the firm three years ago after having obtained his master’s degree in
business administration from a local university. He appears to be very
career driven, having been promoted from an assistant account manager to a
senior account manager in the span of 18 months. He had also authored
several short articles on LinkedIn, including one stating that wealth
managers need to take better advantage of technological tools to enhance
customer service.
      The criminal group ultimately decides to attempt to approach Javier.
They plan to offer a trial version of a new software program said to enhance
an account manager’s ability to identify significant life-changing events of
clients such as marriages, births of children and deaths of close family
members as well as professional milestones such as promotions or awards
received. In addition to highlighting events that would prompt the account
manager to reach out and offer a congratulatory message, it would provide
recommendations for the marketing of additional products and services
related to the event. Additionally, the software would also help identify
individuals closely linked to the client by familiar, social or business ties,
offering considerable potential for surfacing strong market leads that could
leverage potential referrals from current clients.
      The criminal group chooses team member Gary Johnson for this direct
approach due to his background in software development. To decrease
blowback to Gary in the event of a compromise, they decide that he would
develop and use an alias persona for this specific requirement.
      In preparation for this assignment, Gary needs to develop and
memorize his personal alias legend. He comes up with the following:
      Name: Gary Sutherman
      Born: 18 November 1976, Chicago, Illinois
      Civil Status: Single
      Parents: John Sutherman and Cynthia (Prado) Sutherman
      Siblings: One younger brother named Matt
           University Studies: BS in Liberal Arts and MBA from University of
      Illinois at Champagne – Urbana
      Past Employers: Names of two large multinational firms
           Current Employer: Los Angeles-based Quest Innovative Solutions,
      LLC
      Position: Business Development
           Current Residential Address: Montclair Brentwood Apartments,
      11965 Montana Ave, Unit 57, Los Angeles, CA 90049A
      Gary decides to use his first true name, a common practice given that
there is always the possibility of running into someone who knows him. All
the additional information is made up and relatively easy for Gary to
remember and defend. By claiming to have studied and worked at very
large organizations, he made it extremely difficult for anyone to verify.
Using Google Earth, he virtually toured the areas around the University of
Illinois and his ostensible residence in Los Angeles for basic familiarity in
the event he ran into someone who was familiar with these areas. With the
exception of his current employer’s identity of Quest, he felt comfortable in
defending this basic level of cover without any need of backstopping.

name of Quest. He set up a functioning business email address and paid
cash for a non-attributable prepaid cell phone with an area code from the
greater Los Angeles metropolitan area. He contracted with a local business

company and offer to take messages for him.
      With the basic structure in place, Gary must now undertake a more
laborious task. He will need to develop a robust working knowledge of the
wealth management industry, along with the services they provide to their
clients. It is paramount that he appears legitimate during his initial
encounter with the target, which requires his use of industry terminology
and a high level of confidence. Gary is a quick study and can procure most
of this industry background via research on the internet. Time permitting,
he might even try to attend an industry event to obtain a sense of how
industry professionals conduct themselves—he would like to emulate Javier
as much as possible.
      If Javier is comfortable with this first encounter, the unsuspecting
target will likely do little more than conduct a cursory review of Quest’s
website. If Javier wants to dig one level deeper, he might try calling Gary’s
company, and would feel reassured when the call is answered in the name
of the company by the receptionist working for the business service center.

regularly attends. As the chapter is always interested in potential new

      After the speaker gives her presentation and near the end of the

arrange for a demo while Javier would obtain the support of key officials in

     Javier enthusiastically takes the thumb drive and inserts it into his

message on his cell phone, and in the coming days will feel surprised and
puzzled that Gary never returns his call. Within three days of the attempted
installation, Gary’s criminal group will extract 1.3 terabytes of data from
Javier’s employer.

Leveraging Psychology for Manipulation:
      Trust and Influence Factors
         “Trust is like blood pressure. It’s silent, vital to goo health, and if abused it can be
     deadly""

                                          —Frank Sonnenberg, author of Follow Your Conscience




         “When it comes to controlling human beings, there is no better instrument than lies.
     Because you see, humans live by beliefs. And beliefs can be manipulated.”

                                                                                 —Michael Ende




Social  engineering, by definition, is the ability to manipulate another
human to undertake an action that may not be to their benefit but will serve
to accomplish the objectives of the social engineer. As noted in previous
chapters, collecting information on the targeted individual will help
determine which motivations or vulnerabilities particular to that individual
can be leveraged in the approach. Social engineers also take advantage of
additional psychological factors that make most people susceptible to
manipulation.
    Trust
    The concept of trust is extremely powerful. Trust has existed since the
dawn of civilization and serves to bring people together to form societies.
Even the early cavemen formed small groups of people who trusted in one
another for the purpose of survival. Trusting in others is considered crucial
for people’s wellbeing—without it, humanity would see the breakdown of
world order and utter chaos. However, the main character trait that makes
humans very vulnerable to social engineering techniques is the tendency to
blindly trust everyone, even people they do not know. As I was quick to
discover in my career, this is especially true in American culture.
      Like many Americans, I was brought up by my parents to trust
everyone, until a person gave me a reason not to. Unfortunately, I had to go
through several painful experiences to realize that I needed to better temper
my trust. Many of these lessons were taught early in my business career
during interactions with international executives. On the surface, they
appeared as friendly and genuine as anyone else. Later, after I got burned, I
learned that some people in other cultures only trust those within their inner
circle, including family members and close friends. Anyone else was fair
game to take advantage of, and this was considered perfectly normal.
      While trust is often influenced by regional and cultural factors,
Americans need look no further than their own history to identify several
famous fraudsters who successfully preyed upon their victims’ trust to
swindle massive amounts of money. One recent notorious case was Bernie
Madoff. He convinced more than 4,800 wealthy individuals to entrust their
money in his investment Ponzi scheme, and ultimately defrauded them of
approximately $65 billion until his arrest in 2008. This scheme was
originally named after Charles Ponzi, who infamously bilked investors out
of more than $20 million in the early 1900s. One of my favorite fraudster
stories is that of Charles Parker, who successfully got investors to buy
several famous landmarks from him, including the Brooklyn Bridge.
Parker’s scheme was the origin of the expression “If you believe that, I have
a bridge to sell you!”
      As an intelligence officer, I found that garnering and building trust was
exceptionally easy for me. When initiating contact, I would try to leverage
the preestablished trust that the target likely had in the individual or entity I
was associating myself with. Whether true or contrived, I would frequently
say that this trusted person or organization highly recommended that I
speak with the target—as salespeople know, referrals are very powerful.
Even if the target perceives no personal benefit in meeting with you, they
will frequently accommodate the request in order not to burn any bridges
with whoever recommended them.
      A few specific examples illustrate how a social engineer can structure
and deliver a referral that appears to come from the Association of Cyber
Intelligence Professionals (ACIP) to their member, Susan Catherwood,
Chief of Operations at Dynamic Winds, LLC.
      ➢        Place a call to ACIP and ask if they can provide contact
         information regarding one of their members you are interested in
         potentially contracting. You believe the company’s name is
         Dynamic Winds. The association would likely be able and willing
         to provide such information, as they exist to serve and promote their
         members’ businesses.
      ➢      Telephone Susan Catherwood and comment that during a recent
         conversation with ACIP President Doug Frey, he mentioned the
         name of her company.
      ➢         Telephone Susan Catherwood and comment that she was
         recommended by the secretary of ACIP.
      ➢      Call Susan Catherwood and comment that your online research
         regarding the industry indicated that she was highly recommended
         as an expert in her field. In fact, her name even surfaced several
         months ago when speaking with an (unidentified) member of ACIP.
      While none of these referrals are genuine, they would likely be
accepted and facilitate a conversation with Susan. Unless she became very
suspicious of the social engineer’s demeanor or proposition, Susan is
unlikely to attempt to verify the referral. She automatically trusts the social
engineer’s explanation regarding the source of the referral.
      As previously mentioned in Chapter 2, social engineers will always
identify the universities from which potential targets have graduated. Many
people feel a strong bond with their alma mater, and it can be a great ploy
for initiating contact.
      Leveraging well-formulated pretexts or referrals from trusted parties is
great for obtaining the initial meeting with a target. After that, the social
engineer must use their interpersonal skills to build additional trust. Once in
the target’s presence, it was always important for me to greet them with a
firm handshake, a warm smile and periodic direct eye contact. I needed to
connect on a human level, quickly learning about what was important to
them and then relating a personal anecdote, whether genuine or contrived,
that would serve to create a common bond and lead to rapport. I found
incorporating humor was always beneficial as it served to put them at ease
and lower their guard. Being able to speak in their native language and
discuss current events of interest was always an added plus.
      While I spent considerable time preparing and rehearsing my cover
pretext in advance of an initial encounter, it was never foolproof. That said,
I was amazed at how frequently people would dismiss something that may
have surfaced during our interaction that could have called into question my
legitimacy. Perhaps it was a lack of knowledge in specific industry
terminology or an inability to adequately address a question posed. While it
might appear strange to the target when it occurs, they frequently rationalize
it as only a minor inkling that something is not quite right. Ultimately, they
want to trust you and will do everything they can to justify it.
      Social engineers use a variety of techniques to exploit their victims’
trust and pretend to be someone other than themselves. Without trying to
verify, these victims readily accept what they see, read and hear. Only
afterwards do they lament that they were so easily deceived. Unfortunately,
the price to pay for that lesson is typically millions of dollars in financial
losses for their organization, or their own personal financial ruin.

     Trust almost always lays the groundwork for a successful interaction
with a targeted individual. But in addition to leveraging trust, professional
social engineers will also use the following influence techniques.
     Liking
     As victims are more likely to provide assistance to someone they find to
be pleasant, the social engineer will attempt to develop strong personal
rapport prior to making the request. This approach can begin during the
initial interaction by being very pleasant, maintaining eye contact, smiling
and perhaps demonstrating that they have something in common, which
helps form a bond. For example, if I have seen on the individual’s social
media profile or a diploma displayed in their workspace that they went to
the University of Wisconsin, I can pretend to have visited Wisconsin. I can
easily discuss having experienced extreme cold, going to a Green Bay
Packers game or enjoying beer and brats. I immediately develop a bond
with this person.
     Authority
     Victims are more likely to comply if they believe that the request is
coming from someone in authority or representing authority figures such as
the boss, building security or law enforcement. People tend to not question
the request or whether it is rational—they feel an obligation to immediately
comply, especially if they believe that failure to comply may result in
negative consequences for them. For example, if approached by someone
dressed in a security uniform, most people would not question the
legitimacy of instructions given to them.
    Reciprocation
    After the social engineer has conducted a small or low-stakes kind deed
for the target, the latter will often automatically reciprocate by performing a
similar kind deed for the social engineer. A very basic example is holding
open the first set of double doors for someone coming behind you. Almost
certainly, that person will walk through and then hold open the second set
of doors for you to pass through.
    Obligation
    Victims will often feel a strong sense of obligation to help the social
engineer after having a significant courtesy or favor extended to them.
Examples of this could include the social engineer helping the individual
with a problem and then subsequently asking them for a favor. Other
examples may be a sense of obligation felt after having been taken out to
lunch by, or receiving a gift from, the social engineer.
    Concession
    Here the social engineer will ask for something that the victim initially
finds implausible to comply with. The victim will subsequently agree to
comply with a request from the social engineer that appears to be meeting
halfway. This type of interaction can be as straightforward as the following
example:
      Receptionist: Sir, you must have an appointment for authorization to
proceed to the executive floor.
      Social engineer: I understand. I will come back after setting one up.
However, I previously spoke with Mr. Grim and mentioned that I would be
sending product information to his attention. I understand that he will be
leaving on extended travel later today. I really need to get this information
to him. Can I just drop this off with his executive assistant? I will be in and
out in less than five minutes. You can even retain my driver’s license if you
would like.
      Receptionist: Okay, no problem. You can leave your driver’s license
with me.
    Scarcity
    The social engineer can put pressure on the victim to make a quick
decision as the window of opportunity for the victim is about to close. This
tactic is frequently used in sales gimmicks such as “while supplies last,”
“going fast” or “only two more left.” As a result, the target makes the
decision without an opportunity to obtain further information for proper
evaluation.
    Consensus or Social Proof
    The social engineer may place pressure upon the target by implying that
refusing to assist will be seen by others as socially unacceptable. Many of
us have personally experienced this at a checkout counter when the cashier
asks aloud, in the presence of other customers, whether we want to
contribute to a particular charity. You feel like everyone is waiting to hear
your response, and you might feel greater pressure to contribute compared
to having received a similar solicitation through the mail.
      These influence techniques can be used in a variety of situations,
including brief one-time interactions with a target.

There are typically three different ways to extract information from people
that are used in very different situations. The first is interrogation—asking
direct questions of the detained interrogee to extract critical information.
This is typically considered a very hostile approach to information
extraction and may include the use of torture or sleep deprivation.
      The second method is referred to as an interview or debrief. The
individual posing the questions is in a position of authority and asks
specific questions of the interviewee regarding a specific topic. This could
be an interview conducted by the director of security of various employees
following a security breach. It could be a debrief of an intelligence source
by his case officer. It could be an interview by HR of a candidate interested
in filling a new position. In such situations, the interviewee is not detained
and completely understands the context of the questioning.
      The third method, called elicitation, is the acquisition of information
from a person while not disclosing the real intent of the interview or casual
conversation. Elicitation is one of the most important tools used by social
engineers attempting to breach security because they can control the
conversation and get the target to talk while effectively listening. The social
engineer can elicit information whether they’re bumping into a target at his
favorite local café, sitting next to her on the airplane or targeting him at an
industry trade show. Elicitation is not only conducted during face-to-face
encounters, it is also used by social engineers in various online
communication platforms as well as by phone.
     As an intelligence officer, I received extensive training in elicitation
techniques that I used throughout my CIA career. What information was I
after? The same information that other intelligence officers and criminals
tend to seek—it might be information regarding the target organization that
is not readily available and would help me get closer to my objective of
breaching their security. It might be the identification of key insiders in the
organization who have access to the information I wanted to steal, or it
might be information that helped me understand the security vulnerabilities
of that organization’s operations. In many cases, it included specific
confidential or proprietary information that officials were not authorized to
disclose.
     There are several successful elicitation techniques I used most often in
my career. Keep in mind that anyone could have these approaches used
against them—especially if they are approached by someone who they do
not already have a relationship with.
    Closed versus Open Questioning
    Closed questions are those which typically seek a very short response,
such as a “yes” or “no.”
     Elicitor: Does your current position require you to travel overseas?
     Target: Yes, I travel periodically for my job.
    Open questions are asked in such a way to elicit responses that will go
beyond a few short words. They will require the target to go into detail.
     Elicitor: What would you say are your favorite and least favorite
overseas places that you have had to travel for your job?
     Target: My favorite is travel to Turkey. I love the food and the Turkish
people. It also allows me to practice my Turkish. My least favorite are trips
to China. It takes so much longer to get there. Unfortunately, I don’t speak
any Chinese and I really have not had any opportunity to sightsee. I spend
all my time in meetings or at the hotel.
    Flattery
    Everyone loves to be complimented, whether on their clothing,
appearance, professional success or family.
      Elicitor: Wow, you appear to have progressed very well in your
position and could serve as a great inspiration for those just getting started
in this profession. What would you say were your main keys to success?
      Target: Oh, you are so kind. I would like to attribute it all to my own
efforts. However, I was very lucky to work several years under the tutelage
of Mr. Brian Williams before he moved onto General Electric as CEO. He is
brilliant.
    Exploit the Instinct to Complain
    Everyone has pent up frustrations, whether they are with their
coworkers, bosses or significant others. They will unlikely go directly to
their boss and tell him what an idiot he is. However, they will welcome a
release. If probed, they are likely to share their frustrations with someone
they believe they can trust not to disclose their comments.
      Elicitor: What are some of the frustrating challenges you have when
working with your senior management?
      Target: I generally get along with everyone, except with my direct
supervisor. She seems to take direct personal credit for any success by our
unit and shifts blame to her subordinates for any failures. I’m sort of tired
of making her look good.
    Word Repetition
    By repeating back the same word or phrase that the target has used, the
elicitor will usually create a need for the target to provide further
elaboration.
      Target: Last year our sales were down by 54 percent.
      Elicitor: Down 54 percent?
      Target: Yes, this is very unusual after six straight years of strong
growth and is largely attributable to the rollout of a similar product by
Chinese firm Huawei.
    Quotation of Reported Facts
    Here the elicitor is drawing upon something that was already reported in
the press.
      Elicitor: I read in the Wall Street Journal that several of the largest
U.S. banks were having difficulty in complying with the new U.S. Treasury
reporting requirements identifying offshore accounts held on behalf of their
customers.
      Target: Yes. As you can imagine, confidentiality for our clients is a
delicate matter. If we cannot ensure confidentiality, some of the clients will
look to other financial institutions, which tend to circumvent these
requirements.
    Naiveté
    When the elicitor expresses a lack of understanding about something
they have experienced or learned about, it encourages the target to be
helpful as a mentor to the elicitor on the subject.
      Elicitor: I just don’t understand how trade policy decisions like raising
tariffs on imported Chinese goods will help your company increase its sales
in the domestic market.
      Target: Currently, our closest competitors are from China due to their
very low labor costs. A 25 percent increase in tariffs would force these
competitors to either lower their profit margins or increase their local
prices.
    Criticism
    Here the elicitor will criticize something that the target has an opinion
on. It will allow for the target to share the same criticism or to provide their
own view, which might serve as insight.
      Elicitor: The company’s extensive research and development activities
into self-driven vehicles is an absolute waste of resources. This will never
be successful.
      Target: Actually, they have conducted a lot of surveys on this topic.
The survey results tend to indicate that most people, especially those
between the ages of 18 and 35, acknowledge that they would be open to
using this technology as long as certain safety standards are met.
    Feigned or Real Disbelief
    While somewhat similar to the quotation of reported facts, here the
elicitor will express dismay or disbelief that the reported information is true
in an attempt to get the target to explain that it is true and why.
      Elicitor: There is no way that the firm’s latest acquisition of Alliance
Technologies will be profitable.
      Target: Actually, it does not appear profitable if we only look at the
balance sheets. However, the real goal is to eliminate one of the biggest
competitors in our market. We will then be able to improve upon our profit
margins by increasing our prices while lowering many of our production,
distribution and marketing costs.
     Purposefully Erroneous Statement
     The elicitor will make up a false statement and attribute it to something
they may have heard or read, and the target will usually take the opportunity
to set the record straight.
      Elicitor: I heard a news commentator state yesterday that United
Airlines was having severe financial difficulty and was in the process of
filing for Chapter 7 bankruptcy.
      Target: You must have heard something wrong. This is impossible.
United is very healthy financially. In fact, they just placed an order last
week for an additional 35 aircraft from Boeing.
     Mirroring Personal Discussion
     This approach is exceptionally useful when trying to obtain basic
information from someone who the elicitor has just met.
      Elicitor: I can’t wait to get back home. I have already missed my son’s
last two soccer games and my daughter’s piano recital. And you, do you
have kids?
      Target: Oh yes, I have two boys and one girl.
      Elicitor: Kids are great. How old are they?
     “My Friend John”
     Here the elicitor will make up a story that they heard and attribute it to a
friend or family member who ostensibly made the comment.
      Elicitor: My brother-in-law, John, works as a researcher for Magnum
Defense Industries. He says that his company recently adopted many
intrusive policies affecting their employees. They must receive prior
authorization before travelling overseas and report all their foreign
contacts. They are also subject to random searches of their briefcases and
handbags. Is this normal in your industry?
      Target: It varies from company to company. We don’t require
employees to obtain approval prior to travel. However, they must file a
report after returning from such a trip. It includes reporting any foreign
contacts made with whom they plan to have a sustained relationship.
                                        ◆◆◆
    There are additional considerations for social engineers regarding
elicitation and the use of these techniques. First, while very effective, no
technique should be overused. It is best for the elicitor to make use of mix
of techniques during a conversation in order to avoid any suspicion from
the target.
      Prior to each planned encounter with a target, I would map out the
goals of my conversation. This would usually include five or six specific
points I wanted to gather information on regarding the individual or their
organization. For each of the points, I looked at what specific elicitation
technique I could use to introduce the topic. I then considered how I could
manage a logical flow of the overall conversation to cover all points. It
never ceased to amaze me on how easy it was to elicit very sensitive
information from people who barely knew me.
      Enhanced opportunities for elicitation can be leveraged when
conversing with the target in a variety of venues to include their office or
residence as well as other locations far removed from either. In the case of
the office and residence, targets will have artifacts on display, which present
insight into what is important to them while offering a convenient pretext
on which to ask a question. At the office, the target will likely have pictures
of their children as well as framed accomplishments such as university
degrees, professional certifications and other recognitions. The elicitor may
also experience how this individual interacts with their boss or staff.
      At the residence, the elicitor may experience the target’s interactions
with their spouse and children. He will be able to assess the social status of
the target as well as identify hobbies and other interests.
      At a third location removed from both home and office—especially in
more social settings—the target may be much more relaxed and talk about
their feelings relating to their professional and family situation. And of
course, the more alcohol consumed, the more talkative the targets can
become.
      A key determinant regarding whether a target will share sensitive
information during an elicitation is whether or not they consider the
information to be truly protected. The signing of nondisclosure agreements
and clear marking of information as restricted confidential information will
likely resonate and impede targets from disclosing it. However, if the
organization does not have in place a system to classify information, the
employee might
not appreciate the true value of the information for an adversary.

The extensive work that is conducted to select a target entity, collect open-
source intelligence on that entity, identify potential insiders and establish
contact with them is only one portion of a social engineer’s scheme. Now
that they have built an assessment profile or even gained the trust of the
target, it’s time to use various communication channels to effectively
manipulate them.
      These channels are best categorized into the following four
approaches: email (phishing), SMS text messages (smishing), voice call
(vishing) and good old-fashioned face-to-face encounters. Phishing and
smishing, the most common digital manipulation tools, require a similar
carefully crafted yet passive approach. Vishing and face-to-face encounters
will be discussed in Chapter 8.
    Phishing
    According to cybersecurity experts, email phishing is currently the most
prevalent channel used by social engineers to breach security. An attacker
sends an email to his intended victims containing a link or attachment that
the recipient is encouraged to open. Clicking on the link may lead to
downloading malicious code or prompting the recipient to disclose login
information such as usernames, passwords, pin codes, account numbers or
other sensitive data. Likewise, the opening of the attachment may release
malware into the computer. The typical phisher will design an email that
can be sent to thousands of potential victims. The email will appear to come
from an institution that most members of the public may already have a
relationship with.
      One common approach to phishing involves an email alert from a
financial institution such as Bank of America. Since a large percentage of
the population has an account with Bank of America, most recipients will
take note of an email that appears to be coming from that bank. Typically,
such an email will advise of an alert regarding suspicious activity on the
recipient’s account, claiming that the account is frozen until the transaction
can be verified. The email will contain a link for the recipient to click to
directly log into their account. Once the recipient fills in the fields of
username, password and additional security information such as account
number, date of birth or social security number, the phisher has all the
information they need to immediately take over the victim’s account.
      Basing a phishing email on ecommerce is another common tactic.
Almost everyone has made purchases on Amazon at some point, so the
phisher sends out a blanket email with confirmation of the processing and
shipment of an order. The email contains a PDF attachment with the full
details. If and when the recipient clicks on the link to open the attachment,
malicious code is introduced into the computer.
      Another common tactic is masquerading as a popular online service
such as Netflix. The phisher sends out an email appearing to come from
Netflix stating that payment was declined from the credit card on file for the
account. The email contains a link to update payment information and
requires entry of the user’s full name, credit card number, expiration date
and security code. Once again, the phisher has all they need to compromise
the credit card.
      In addition to these traditional examples, social engineers will also
take advantage of recent events that are captivating the general public. At
the time of this writing, the world was experiencing fear and panic
regarding the spread of COVID-19. There were many cases of phishing
emails sent out by criminal organizations leveraging the crisis, targeting
anxious and unsuspecting recipients and claiming to provide updated
information. Each of these emails provided a few introductory paragraphs
along with a link for additional information. Unfortunately, many
individuals fell victim by clicking on such links.
    Spear Phishing
    I occasionally encounter people who will comment that they are not so
naïve as to fall for these mass-marketed gimmicks. While congratulating
them on their basic email hygiene, I then caution them that phishing emails
are not always so basic. In fact, professional social engineers looking to
breach a high-value target will often deploy spear phishing methodology.
      Compared to basic phishing, which is like casting nets from all sides of
the boat and playing the odds that several fish will likely be caught, the
carefully crafted “spear” is flung with exceptional precision, almost
guaranteeing that the intended target will fall victim. Why is it so precise?
The email was crafted from the target’s personality assessment profile,
which was formulated from online social media data. The investment in
time taken to research the target and design this approach is minuscule
when criminals are looking at netting thousands, if not millions, of dollars
from the breach. Spear phishing attacks have become increasingly common
in a variety of industries.
      To illustrate this approach, say some criminals have decided to breach
Diamond Utility Corporation (DUC) to obtain data on their customers.
After careful study, they have decided to target Randy Wilton, who is
DUC’s senior data analyst. When gathering data for his personality
assessment profile, they learn that Randy has a passion for collecting rare
stamps, coins and documents. Based on this assessment, they send him an
email announcing the upcoming auction of an authentic copy of the
Declaration of Independence. While 200 were printed on July 4, 1776, only
26 copies survive today. Randy’s knee-jerk reaction will be to click on this
link, not thinking that it might be a phishing attempt. Once he clicks on the
link, the criminal group compromises his computer and eventually his
company’s entire network. After stealing customer data, the criminals
pursue a bonus by installing ransomware, effectively shutting down the
power supply to these customers until DUC pays the requested $3.1 million
ransom.
      Another example would be if a foreign intelligence service was
looking for a way to breach the security of the U.S. Department of Defense
(DOD). They would assess that targeting a new state-of-the-art DOD
facility being built over the next 18 months might give them an opportunity.
While directly penetrating this facility would be next to impossible, they
decide to look at several of the outsourced firms contracted for the buildout.
They identify Kline Engineering Consultancy Services (KECS) as a
potential target. Within KECS, they identify CEO William Kline as their
priority inside target, and considerable information on the CEO is gathered
from social media accounts and other online sources. His personality profile
assessment reveals that one of his top passions is sailing—he often posts
pictures and comments highlighting outings with family and friends on his
250-foot sailboat. On more than one occasion, he has commented that one
of his dreams is to someday sail the Mediterranean.
      The intelligence service decides to craft an email appearing to come
from his local sailing club. The email announces an upcoming sailing
excursion to the Mediterranean with limited availability, and attachments
are said to contain additional information such as pricing and registration
forms. Although he has received basic phishing awareness training, William
immediately downloads the attachments for more information. In addition
to believing that he can trust an email appearing to come from his local
sailing club, he does not want to miss out on an opportunity to pursue his
lifelong dream. As a result, William’s laptop is immediately compromised
with malware. In addition to storing sensitive information on this laptop,
William also uses it to access his firm’s server. The foreign intelligence
service is now one step closer to achieving its objective of breaching the
DOD’s new facility.
      Another variation of a spear phishing email would be the social
engineer’s creation of a fictitious LinkedIn account to engage a target,
Robert Franklin, a senior project manager for the Allied Health Network
(AHN). From his LinkedIn profile, the criminals learned that Robert is a
member of the Freemasons. Given the fraternal nature of Freemasons, they
assess that Robert will likely accept an invitation to connect coming from
someone who claims to be a fellow member. Once they have connected and
exchanged a couple of messages, the social engineer sends an email to
Robert with an attachment and asks him to open and review the attachment.
Robert trustingly opens the attachment, releasing malware and infecting
AHN with ransomware. As AHN cannot afford any delay in accessing
patient records and wants to avoid any blowback to their public reputation,
AHN agrees to pay $6.5 million in bitcoin to secure the decryption code.
      I occasionally come across someone who tells me, “Nah, most people
will not accept invitations on LinkedIn from people they do not know.”
Based upon my own personal experience with LinkedIn, I would strongly
argue that most people do accept such invitations. Frequently, they will not
even bother to review the LinkedIn profile of the requester—they are only
interested in expanding the numbers in their network. Furthermore, if a
professional social engineer is concerned that the target might not accept
such a request, they can first invite people from the target’s network to
connect before extending the invitation. Such connections include
coworkers, industry peers or members of the same professional
organization. Once the target sees that several people from their own
network are already connected to this fictitious account holder, they will
likely accept.
     LinkedIn is not the only social media platform that can be used to
deliver a spear phishing attack. Practically any platform that allows users to
send and receive private messages can be leveraged to deliver malicious
links or attachments. That includes Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp,
Twitter and WeChat, just to name a few. Due to the general public’s
increasing use and trust in social media networks, along with the ease in
creating fake profiles for anonymity, such platforms will likely replace
email as the channel of choice for professional social engineers.
    Smishing
    Back in early 2002 when I was living in France, I was amazed by the
popularity of text messaging throughout Western Europe. While it was not
so common in the U.S. at that time, it was only a few more years until it
eventually became one of the principal means of communication in
America. In some cases, it is even a more effective way of communicating
than traditional email. People receive dozens, if not hundreds of emails on
any given day. Unable to read them all, they will typically read the title
lines before deciding to read, ignore or delete. Text messages, however,
seem to get much more attention. In addition to receiving fewer of them,
many people will have alerts activated for incoming text messages. As a
result, they are likely to take a closer look at the contents of each text
message received. Marketing companies have recently incorporated the use
of text messages, fully realizing that they have a much higher click-through
rate than standard emails. Social engineers, also realizing this trend, have
now incorporated smishing into their toolkit.
     In the context of social engineering, smishing is actually very similar
to phishing. However, instead of using an email, the social engineer will
send a link or document file via a text message. The result is the same.
Once the link is clicked, or document file opened, the attacker has breached
the security of that mobile device. Smishing is named for the SMS
messages used to carry out the scheme.
     Say a criminal group was able to purchase on the dark web a digital
employee telephone directory for Maximus Worldwide Travel Services
(MWTS). They develop and send to several employees over a three-day
holiday weekend a text message appearing to come from MWTS’s HR
department. It advises that there is a potential technical problem regarding
the upcoming payroll, and recipients are instructed to immediately open the
attachment, which will provide information on what they need to do to
avoid a disruption in their pay. Playing upon emotion and the sense of
urgency in avoiding a delay in pay, most of the recipients will immediately
open the attachment. Upon doing so, they will compromise their company-
issued mobile devices, which are also used to access MWTS servers.
Because the attack happened over a holiday weekend, it will take several
hours before MWTS management will realize that there may be a
compromise.
     Smishing is especially effective against people who use their mobile
devices to conduct the bulk of their day-to-day communications. Rebecca
Miller is the owner of one of the largest real estate agencies in South
Florida, and she is constantly dealing with established clients and frequently
receives referrals from them. After conducting minimal research, the social
engineer easily obtains her mobile telephone number and the names of
several former clients. He then crafts a text message falsely claiming that he
was referred to Rebecca by one of her clients. The text message includes a
word document identifying a listing of interest. Rebecca downloads this
document and never realizes that it contained malware, which allows the
social engineer to eventually gain access to her firm’s database. The prize is
information on the financial transactions of clients for the previous 10
years.
     Phishing and smishing are passive communication tools sent by social
engineers to their intended victims for the purpose of enticing them to
undertake the simple action of clicking on a link or opening an attachment
that will download malware. In addition to compromising the victim’s
computer or mobile device, these tools can potentially compromise an
entire network.

Vishing
    Compared to phishing and smishing activity, which is considered
passive communication, vishing is an active and dynamic social
engineering technique that uses the telephone to directly engage with the
target. While vishing will seldom directly result in a security breach, it can
play an important, and sometimes crucial, role in executing more elaborate
security breach attempts. The purpose of the call may be to get the victim to
divulge sensitive information such as internal processes or procedures, or it
may be conducted to persuade the victim to undertake a specific action,
such as modification of their account settings or creation of access passes.
      Think of vishing as making a pretext call. To accomplish the objective,
social engineers must successfully convince recipients that we are whoever
we pretend to be. To increase credibility, social engineers will often spoof
the call—using simple technology to manipulate the caller ID seen by the
recipient. Using spoofing, I could assume a number of roles when calling a
target, such as a fellow employee located at another facility, someone from
tech support, the target’s investment adviser or someone from the school
where target’s children attend.
      Vishing can take many forms. Say a criminal group is attempting to
steal customer data from Quantum Entertainment (QE), a leading provider
of online media content. While evaluating several potential insiders, they
notice an update to the LinkedIn profile of Christine Belasco, who recently
started as a receptionist at QE. Assessing that Christine is not yet familiar
with QE policies and procedures and likely isolated from coworkers, they
decide to use a vishing approach. The social engineer pretends to be Tom
from the IT support team and spoofs the caller ID so that it appears to be
legitimately coming from that department. Tom welcomes Christine to the
company and encourages her to reach out to IT for any questions or
assistance as needed. He then advises that an anomaly has been detected
within her user profile, which will eventually result in a crash. Tom offers
to assist Christine by rebuilding the user profile at her convenience.
Christine, fearing how a crash could negatively impact her performance at
this new job, immediately takes Tom up on the offer. Tom sends her an
email with a link to authorize login access to her account. While keeping
Christine distracted on the phone, Tom uses this access to create a backdoor
into the network, which will be exploited later by his team. Upon
conclusion of the 15-minute call, Christine expresses her appreciation to
Tom for helping to prevent what could have been a serious disaster for her,
not realizing that she had just facilitated a breach that will result in
devastating consequences for QE.
      Vishing attempts can get exceedingly complex if the social engineer
has access to information about multiple targets within an organization.
Consider a criminal group that is interested in breaching the security of the
Global Health Foundation (GHF) for information on more than 140,000
wealthy donors who contribute a significant portion of its $230 million
annual budget. Based upon open-source research, the social engineer has
learned that GHF CEO Alex Burton is currently away on travel to Dubai to
attend the 14th World Congress on Healthcare in Developing Countries. A
complete conference schedule was available online. Research also revealed
that when travelling abroad, senior GHF officials always contract a security
transport company by the name of Liberty Limo. The social engineer
decides to design and execute a pretext call claiming to be from Liberty
Limo and requests to speak with Alex’s executive secretary, Kimberly
Davis. The objective is to get Kimberly to transmit very sensitive data,
believing that she is complying with a request from her boss.
      Due to the eight-hour time difference between Dubai and GHF
headquarters in New York, the social engineer places a call to Kimberly at
0830 (EST). He spoofs the call to show “Liberty Limo” on the caller ID.
      Ms. Kimberly? (spoken with a slight middle eastern accent)
      Yes?
     This is Rashid from Liberty Limo. We are handling Mr. Alex’s transport
and other needs while he is here in Dubai. Don’t worry—he is very fine. He
just went into an important meeting and asked me to contact you. He
urgently needs a copy of the latest report detailing the names of GHF’s top
financial benefactors, with their names, amounts donated and their contact
information. Mr. Alex requested that it be sent to my email address so that I
can immediately print it out and pass to him during his meeting. He is
having some problems receiving incoming emails to his phone.
     What is the email address he wants it sent to?
     Rashid.LibertyLimo@gmail.com

     To further alleviate any concern or suspicion that Kimberly may have,
the social engineer further adds:

      Ms. Kimberly, Mr. Alex also wants to know what your favorite color is.
      My favorite color? What in heaven’s name for?
      Well, I think he found something from the UAE that he wants to bring
back. I can’t tell you what it is. I only need your favorite color.
      He shouldn’t bother bringing anything back for me. My favorite color
is purple.
      Excellent. I will make sure he knows that. Thanks for the email—I just
got it and will immediately pass the report to him.
    Vishing with Chat Groups
    While not typically recognized as a vishing tool, online chat groups can
also be used for the same purpose. One of my university students was
finding it very difficult to collect open-source information on her target
company. However, she then discovered an online chat group where people
freely exchanged information regarding their experiences working for the
firm. Using an alias, my student entered the chat posing as someone who
was very interested in applying for a position that she had seen on the
company’s website. Once she connected with a person who just happened
to be a current employee from the firm’s HR department, she requested that
they move their chat over to a private room. She artfully used a number of
the elicitation techniques previously discussed to collect considerable
information, including sensitive insight into an exploitable vulnerability.
    Face-to-Face Encounters
    Out of all social engineering techniques, in-person engagement was the
approach I most favored during my career as an intelligence officer. It
allowed me to fully leverage nonverbal communication, which is
nonexistent in either phishing or smishing and rather limited during vishing.
For me, effective nonverbal communication included warm handshakes,
smiles, direct eye contact and lots of arm gestures.
     Likewise, it allowed me to see invaluable nonverbal cues the target
displayed, which would help me measure progress in rapport building and
gauge their comfort level with me. More importantly, it would help to better
determine their comfort level regarding the information I was eliciting, or
the action I requested they take on my behalf. It helped me visualize
whether I had a green, yellow or red light. If green, I could push forward
and strike while the iron was hot. If yellow, I knew that I would have to
take a step back and attempt to better frame the discussion to put them at
greater ease. If red, I would have to back away, change the subject and
consider other options.
     The respective objectives for a social engineer’s use of face-to-face
encounters can be numerous, but this approach is used most effectively in a
few common scenarios.
    Facility Intrusions
    To gain unescorted access into a target facility, the social engineer will
need to develop and artfully execute a suitable pretext to get past
gatekeepers such as receptionists, guards and electronic barriers. A common
technique is piggybacking or tailgating—tagging along with another person
or group of people who are authorized to enter the restricted area.
     More calculated pretexts rely upon previously collected information
identifying contracted third-party service providers, as well as those
establishments making deliveries to the facility. The following are some
frequently used roles social engineers might pose as to gain access:
     ➢ A member of the contracted food catering service
     ➢ A member of the contracted guard force
     ➢ A member of the contracted janitorial staff
     ➢ Someone working for IT conducting an installation or repair
     ➢ Someone delivering flowers
     ➢      Someone making a food delivery (local establishment or food
        delivery service)
     ➢ Someone making a FedEx or UPS delivery
     In all cases, the social engineer must look and act the part, and props
are critical. If impersonating a third-party vendor like those listed above,
they must wear clothing that matches the profile. Uniforms are relatively
easy to procure on the market or have manufactured. If required, the social
engineer will carry a badge or visitor pass easily fabricated based off
exemplars seen in photographs uploaded to social media by employees
from the workplace. If delivering flowers, they will carry a nice flower
arrangement as well as the name and location of the person the flowers
must be personally delivered to. They will know what occasion the target is
celebrating, such as a birthday or anniversary, based on Facebook posts. If
claiming to be from IT, they will carry a small tool kit.
     What does the social engineer do once inside? Unescorted access
inside a facility offers several options for technical exploitation:
     ➢        Placement of audio devices in conference rooms to pick up
         confidential discussions during meetings.
     ➢ Placement of keyloggers on computers to collect usernames and
         passwords.
     ➢        Distribution of lethal USBs, such as malware-infected USBs
         labeled “Confidential Payroll.” The social engineer will leave each
         one in an area where an employee could pick it up. While some of
         the employees might turn the discovered USB over to the HR
         department, it is almost certain that at least one of them will first
         insert it into their computer system out of curiosity. Once inserted
         and the corrupted file opened, the compromise is initiated.
     I once had a requirement to gain access to a target office for the
purpose of obtaining a floor plan. This plan would be used by a team that
would later conduct a surreptitious entry in the middle of the night to place
electronic eavesdropping equipment. The targeted office occupied the entire
eighth floor of a 10-story office building.
     Using the pretext that I was interested in available office space, I
contacted the building’s management office by telephone. They provided
me with an appointment to visit available office spaces located on the third
and ninth floors. This appointment allowed me to pass security controls and
access the building. Upon reaching the management office, I met with
Rebecca. We discussed the general services provided by building
management, and I requested and obtained a copy of the generic floor plan
structure common to each floor. Rebecca then led me on a tour of the
building, including the two available offices. As we finished on the ninth
floor, I politely wrapped up my visit. I told Rebecca that I would give her a
decision within the next few days. I excused myself to go to the restroom on
that floor prior to seeing myself out of the building.
      Upon exiting the restroom, I took the stairwell down to the eighth floor
and entered the reception area of my target office. I explained to Jason, the
receptionist, that I would likely be a new tenant taking space on the ninth
floor. I added that during my tour, Rebecca was highly complementary of
the design of the office spaces on the eighth floor and mentioned that they
were the best layout in the entire building. As Jason proudly smiled, I asked
whether he could give me a quick tour of their offices, and he readily
obliged. I made frequent references to the beautiful décor and was granted
permission by Jason to take a few pictures. He even introduced me to a
couple of his colleagues as someone who would soon be a new tenant.
         The information I was able to collect during my visit with Rebecca
followed by the tour of the target office was enough to meet my objective.
Based upon my pictures and additional observations, I was able to return to
the generic map provided by Rebecca and insert the additional details.
    Accessing Insider Targets
    While it may be difficult to establish a viable means to contact and meet
insider targets within their workplace, it is quite easy to target them when
they attend outside events, where they are easily approachable and tend to
have their guard down.
      Professional Conferences
      The social engineer will frequently use professional conferences as an
effective way to approach identified insider targets as well as targets of
opportunity. In advance of the event, the social engineer may be able to
obtain a list of entities that will have representatives at the event. Upon
arrival, the social engineer will aggressively work the floor, looking for
name tags of potential targets and orchestrating brief encounters during
lunch or coffee breaks. Once engaged in a conversation, the social engineer
can establish rapport with a potential target and use elicitation techniques to
collect information on the insider and their employer.
      Conference Speakers
      Insiders may be invited as guest speakers during a conference event,
creating a unique opportunity for the social engineer. The speaker’s
biography will be made available, as well as the presentation topic and date
and time of the presentation. In this case, the social engineer will predesign
a specific pretext for contact with the speaker, which will typically take
place at the end of the presentation. While this initial contact is very brief, it
will set the stage for a follow-up meeting. Even if the social engineer does
not physically attend the event, they can use the pretext of having been
present and contact the target later to request an opportunity to meet.
      A variation to targeting a conference speaker is targeting someone who
is teaching a multi-session course, either at a university or on behalf of a
professional association. On a couple of occasions during my CIA career, I
was able to identify well-placed insiders from high-priority target
organizations who were giving lectures. In both cases, I signed up for the
course and arrived at least 20 minutes early on the day of the first session.
While the participants usually do not arrive until a few minutes before class,
the lecturer typically arrives at least 30 minutes early to set up the venue. I
would take advantage of that time to introduce myself and establish initial
personal rapport. I could then gradually reinforce this connection during
class breaks and remaining course sessions.
      Networking Events
      A diligent social engineer will identify social clubs or professional
associations in which their target is a member. Upcoming events can be
used for networking opportunities with the goal of either meeting the target
directly or identifying someone who knows the target and can provide a
referral.
      Social Facilitators
      At times, a social engineer will orchestrate an event with the sole
purpose of getting the target to attend. They will contract with a third party
to ostensibly sponsor a private event and provide the facilitator with an
extensive list of individuals to invite—including the target. The social
engineer will attend the event and use the opportunity to casually start up a
conversation with the insider during the event. As this is a rather elaborate
and costly scheme, it would only be used with hard-to-access priority target
insiders.
      Headhunter or Executive Recruiter Scheme
      Some companies will manage the complete HR process for hiring
personnel, while others may outsource some of the functions to a third
party, often referred to as a headhunter or executive recruiter. A typical
headhunter will search for prospects who might fit the profile of the
candidate sought by the client. They will use several resources such as
resume databases, networks of contacts and LinkedIn. When calling a
prospective candidate, they may provide general background regarding the
client as well as the position, but typically will not reveal the name of the
client until further into the interview and evaluation process.
      Posing as an executive recruiter is one of the best covers used by
successful social engineers. The social engineer will initiate contact with
the target via telephone as a headhunter working to fill a position with an
unidentified client, describing a position that is a step up from the target’s
current job, whether in compensation, title or level of responsibilities. The
social engineer will mention that they were attracted to the target based
upon her impressive resume or profile on LinkedIn and offer the
opportunity to get together for coffee to conduct a basic interview. Even
when targets are not actively looking for new employment, they typically
jump on this opportunity due to a natural curiosity to learn more about the
market and what they might be worth. In their minds, they have nothing to
lose.
      The social engineer uses this interview to collect considerable
information about the target and their employer alike. In some cases, a
target may be pursued for sensitive information relating to a former
employer. In addition to direct interview questions, the social engineer
artfully uses elicitation techniques to desensitize the target regarding the
attempt to obtain protected information.
      While this could be a one-time encounter, it frequently leads to follow-
up meetings over coffee, lunch or dinner. The target might even be offered a
confidential consultancy agreement to provide insight into the industry and
inside information regarding the activities of the target’s employer.
      Such consultancy arrangements can continue for many months, if not
years. They usually terminate when the “client” no longer has a need for
additional information or when the target loses access due to a change in
position or retirement. Typically, the target’s organization never becomes
aware of this devastating and ongoing breach.
      The Consultant Scheme
      Compared to the headhunting scheme, this is less complex but can
yield a similar outcome. Here, the social engineer will typically contact the
target insider by telephone, identifying as someone who works as an
industry consultant. The “consultant” needs particular insight for a new
project that the target may have based upon an alleged referral or their bio
on LinkedIn. This scheme will typically only work on those individuals
who are motivated by mentoring and teaching. As a lure for ongoing
discussions, the social engineer may entice the target with lavish dinners,
entertainment, gifts and even financial compensation.
      The Desperate University Student Scheme
      This is an easy way for a social engineer to directly interview an
insider for information under the pretext of needing to complete a research
paper or thesis. While conducting the interview, the social engineer will
creatively incorporate elicitation techniques to procure sensitive
information. It is typically a one-time encounter and can be conducted in
person or over the telephone. Insiders are often receptive to this ploy
because they remember what it was like to be a university student and want
to help. This is especially the case when the social engineer claims to be
studying at the insider’s alma mater.
      The Honey Pot or Romance Scheme
      While occasionally encountered in the U.S., this scheme is often used
overseas by both intelligence services and criminal groups. Foreign
businessmen who arrive in the country to conduct meetings or conferences
are prime targets. An attractive female, working under the direction of the
master social engineer, will circulate in the vicinity of the target—often in
their hotels or nearby restaurants and bars—and attempt to lure him into a
conversation. The ignited chemistry will typically evolve into sexual
intimacy.
      In addition to eliciting information from pillow talk that is passed on to
the social engineer, this scheme can leave the insider very vulnerable to
extortion attempts. In exchange for regaining control over compromising
material such as photos or videos, the target will have to provide specific
and damaging information about their organization. Another variant to this
scheme would be the social engineer threatening physical harm to the “love
interest” or her family unless the insider agrees to the demands. In either
case, this scheme is quite effective.

How Organizations Can Mitigate the Risk of a
           Security Breach
          “Where my reason, imagination, or interest were not engaged, I would not or could not
     learn.”

                                                                       —Sir Winston Churchill




The types of threat actors that use social engineering, how they choose
their targets, what they are after, how they prepare for an attack and the
different methods they use to manipulate insiders to achieve their goals all
take a variety of forms—each of which can inflict irreparable damage on an
organization. While there are so many variables to the threat of social
engineers, there are steps organizations can take to significantly decrease
the risk of falling victim to such schemes.
    Awareness and Realization
    Developing an awareness within an organization that social engineering
threats do exist is a starting point to defending against them. However, just
as important as awareness is a genuine realization that no one person or
organization is immune from becoming the target of a breach attempt.
Despite the daily bombardment of news stories regarding massive database
breaches, ransomware attacks and identity thefts, many organizations and
individuals believe that it will never happen to them.
      This mindset is extremely dangerous and makes individuals as well as
organizations very vulnerable. Essentially, they become soft targets. For
criminals, there is no reason to pursue harder or more secure targets when
soft ones abound. Ironically, most soft targets only actively pursue
hardening their security posture after suffering firsthand from an attack,
much like a homeowner waiting for their house to become burglarized
before installing an alarm system. This type of response is reactive instead
of proactive.
      As the human factor is shown to be the weakest link in overall security
and data protection, this is where the greatest focus on prevention needs to
start, from contract employees all the way up to the C-suite. Spending
millions of dollars in technology to harden the physical facility or IT
network is meaningless if human actions, whether intentional or innocent,
circumvent protective controls. This is where security awareness training is
crucial.
      While most organizations have developed security training programs
for regulatory compliance reasons, it frequently ends up being a mere
exercise to check the box. As a result, many organizations look for
inexpensive off-the-shelf, on-demand training programs to meet compliance
requirements. Employees take this annual mandatory training at their desks,
clicking through the program while multitasking and generally find little
personal value in the program. As a result, the process increases a false
sense of security for the organization and reinforces the employee mindset
that security awareness training has little relevance for them.
      True and effective security awareness programs need to start with the
individual employee—it is crucial that they understand and appreciate how
appropriate security measures can personally protect them and their loved
ones from both physical and financial harm. Once they buy into the value of
security at a personal level, they will more readily bring the same awareness
mindset to their role within the company.
      Effective awareness training must extend well beyond conditioning
employees on whether to click on a link in an email or following a checklist
of dos and don’ts. All staff employees and contractors need to fully
understand the bigger picture, taking pride and ownership of their valuable
roles in protecting the organization, its customers and fellow employees.
    Hands-On Training
    After my days of gathering intelligence for the CIA ended, I couldn’t
stop thinking about the ways social engineering techniques are used by
threat actors of all types on ordinary people and organizations. I decided to
put my years of experience to use by training people and organizations to
avoid becoming unwitting insiders like the countless people I targeted in
my career.
      I enjoy conducting interactive educational workshops onsite through
the Counterintelligence Institute and engaging employees on the
information their organization holds that might be of value.
     ➢      What technology, customer information or employee data might
         threat actors seek?
      ➢ Who might want to steal it and for what purpose?
      ➢     Where is that information held within the organization and who
         has access to it?
      ➢ In addition to financial losses from a breach, what impact might it
         have on the brand’s reputation?
      ➢ Might the organization be vulnerable to a ransomware attack?
      ➢ If so, what would the impact on their operations be?
      ➢ In such an event, should they pay the ransom or refuse?
      ➢ Can they imagine scenarios in their company for a business email
         compromise?
      ➢ Might their organization be an attractive target to gain access to a
         customer organization that might be even more alluring?
      I then lead them through a hands-on exercise where they search for
open-source information regarding their organization and discuss the value
of such information to someone planning a breach. This includes the
identification of potential insiders—what departments or employee roles
within the organization could serve as attractive targets? I next focus on the
use of social media for collection on insiders, and I have each employee
conduct a self-assessment of their own social media profile.
      The workshop continues with examples of various social engineering
techniques and a discussion regarding ease or difficulty of their successful
use within their company. Based upon my experience, this approach to
training transforms the way employees think about security and results in
greater awareness, both at the organization as well as in their own personal
lives.
      One of the most important ways an organization can mitigate the risk
of a security breach is by minimizing the amount of information made
publicly available regarding the entity and its employees. All posted
information, whether on the organization’s website or social media sites,
should be evaluated prior to its dissemination to the World Wide Web. Is its
public relations value greater than its potential use by someone trying to
breach the organization’s security? Many times, the answer will be yes.
However, there are also many occasions when the information posted
provides little added value to the organization’s public reputation yet
presents serious vulnerabilities to its security posture.
     In addition to sensitizing employees regarding what information
should not be posted online, the organization should have someone whose
function is to regularly conduct extensive searches on the internet, including
the dark web, to identify anything that could pose a security concern. Once
identified, management will have to ascertain whether the information can
be removed. If it cannot be removed or might remain in an archived version
of the internet, the potential use of this information by nefarious characters
needs to be evaluated, along with suggested safeguards.
     In addition to concerns about controlling the amount of information
available that may be exploitable by social engineers, organizations and
their employees must always be ready to confront attempts to breach
security by recognizing the warning signs.
    Warning Signs: Phishing and Smishing Attempts
    Phishing via email and smishing over SMS messages are the two
communication mechanisms that will often contain links or attached files.
Here are several things to consider:
     ➢ Don’t trust the display name or heading when receiving an email.
         It is more important to focus on the actual email address from which
         it is coming—anyone can copy and paste logos.
     ➢ Realize that email addresses, just like telephone caller IDs, can be
         spoofed.
     ➢ Are you the only recipient, or is there a list of recipients?
     ➢           Is the email addressed specifically to your name, or is it
         generically addressed “Dear Valued Client?”
     ➢        What is the actual content of the message? Is it requesting that
         you take urgent action because of a problem? That is a big red flag.
         Is it asking for personal information? Another huge red flag! Does
         the content make sense? Does it contain grammatical or spelling
         errors? If so, it is likely fraudulent.
     ➢        Review the signature—the lack of details about the signer is an
         indicator of phishing. Companies will not only have a name, but
         also an address, contact phone number, email, web address and
         more.
     ➢         Before clicking on any link, scan URLs to see whether they
         match the name of the source. By hovering your mouse over the
         link, the URL will show on the bottom of the browser or in a pop-
        up box. To test the link, open a new browser window and type in the
        website address directly rather than clicking on the link.
     ➢ You must never click on a link or open an attached file provided
        in any email that you cannot completely trust, especially those
        asking for you to log into your account from that email. You can
        always open a separate browser to directly log into your account
        and ascertain whether the information provided is correct.
        Remember the example of Randy Wilton in Chapter 7 who received
        an email regarding the upcoming auction of a rare document? If
        true, Randy would likely be able to find available information on
        the auction by conducting a simple Google search.
    Warning Signs: Vishing or Face-to-Face Attempts
    The following are potential warning signs of a breach attempt during
phone calls or face-to-face interactions:
     ➢ Refusal by the caller to give a callback number
     ➢      Making an out-of-ordinary request—something not standard or
        normal
     ➢ A claim of authority from a boss, someone representing the boss,
        security or law enforcement
     ➢ A message that stresses urgency and requests that action must be
        taken immediately
     ➢ Someone who threatens negative consequences of noncompliance
        such as jeopardizing your job
     ➢         Someone who shows discomfort when questioned, such as
        hesitating to respond or stuttering
     ➢         Someone that name drops and appears to know everyone,
        including use of referrals
     ➢ The excessive use of compliments or flattery
     ➢ Someone who is inquisitive about you or your employer
     ➢      Asking questions that are out of context of the purpose for the
        meeting or casual encounter
     ➢      The information provided by the person regarding the nature of
        their job or business does not appear fully credible
     In all cases of suspected social engineering, it is extremely important
to verify the identity of the requester as well as the legitimacy of the request
before complying with the action. Remember, they are approaching you
with the request, whether by email, text, telephone or in person. If they have
targeted you, they have done their homework and can be extremely
convincing.
      To verify the authenticity of an interaction, consider the following
steps.
      In the case of email or SMS: Do not automatically click on the link or
open the attachment. If the message was received from an organization, go
directly to their website. In the case of receiving the communication from
an individual, telephone that person.
      In the case of a telephone call: Do not comply with the information
requested. Instruct the caller to make the request in writing. Alternatively,
ask for a callback number and return the call after allowing some time to
pass.
      In the case of someone trying to enter the facility: Follow your
company’s strict policies regarding access to your facility. If they do not
have a proper access badge, direct them to the issuing party. If their badge
requires an escort, do not allow them to proceed without one. If their badge
only allows access to certain areas within the facility, do not allow them to
enter restricted spaces. Make no exceptions. For those issuing the badges,
make sure there is a written request by an authorized official and that it was
received via a secure channel. Never issue one based on a phone call, which
can be susceptible to vishing.
      In the case of being approached by someone during conferences, trade
shows or other locations where you could be targeted: Never discuss
proprietary information with them. Be alert to potential use of elicitation
techniques. Take control of the conversation and use counter-elicitation to
ascertain their credibility. Try to obtain any information from them that may
contain verifiable information (brochure, business card or references).
      If contacted by a headhunter: Before agreeing to personally meet,
request specific information that may be verifiable to include full name,
company name and office telephone number. Google the company’s name
to see whether there is any mention of this company beyond their own
website.
    Incident Reporting and Reinforcing Security Awareness
    Employees should understand that simply not complying with a request
coming from a suspected social engineer is not enough. It is extremely
important that they report the incident to their organization’s security
department, which will be able to track any patterns or trends from incident
reports and help validate whether the organization is the target of a true
attack. When employees fail to report such incidents, they are giving the
attacker another opportunity through someone else.
     Employees need to understand that they are truly the first and best line
of defense for safeguarding the organization. Failure to do so could result in
the loss of trade secrets, customer data, employee data, revenue and
potential loss in employment.
     Consider adapting a slogan that is easy to reinforce and remember,
such as “verify before trust.” This approach should be encouraged, and
employees should never be admonished for taking added measures in
protecting the integrity of the organization’s security.
     To maximize the effectiveness of a security awareness campaign, it is
important that training be reinforced by ongoing practical exercises.
Whether conducted inhouse or through the support of a third-party vendor,
employees need to be randomly tested in all four of the communication
channels used by social engineers, and results and trends need to be
analyzed by management. Individuals who properly shut down the
attempted social engineering ploy need to be recognized and rewarded.
Those who fail should neither be chastised or publicly named. However, it
is important to share the results of these tests with the entire workforce on a
regular basis.

Taking it Home: Safeguarding Your Personal
   Information and Financial Security
          “No one has power over you unless you give it to them, you are in control of your life and
     your choices decide your own fate.”

                                                                                     —Anonymous




Individuals need to be cognizant that they can easily be targeted by a social
engineer for direct personal exploitation or as potential insiders for gaining
access to their targeted organizations. Just as organizations need to exercise
care when disseminating information to the public, individuals have to
exercise that same caution and discipline in not releasing exploitable
information that can make them vulnerable as potential targets.
      The news media regularly reports on major security breaches suffered
by corporations, nonprofit organizations, and academic institutes as well as
city, state and federal government offices. In comparison, there are rarely
reports regarding individuals who have been directly victimized by
ransomware or identify theft. That doesn’t mean such cases are rare,
however—they are just as frequent as successful breaches at organizations.
The difference is that they are not publicly disclosed.
      To put this in perspective, consider the decision process for
organizations and individuals regarding whether to report a security breach.
It boils down to legal requirements—organizations are only required to
report a breach if it resulted in the potential disclosure of personally
identifiable information. These rules are subject to individual state
regulations and will depend upon the location of the breached company, as
well as location of individual consumers who have had their information
compromised. A second regulation comes from the Security and Exchange
Commission (SEC), requiring that all publicly traded companies report the
breach if it has a material impact on shareholders.
      When not required to do so by regulations, an organization will
typically decide not to report. They are concerned that the disclosure of
security breaches will negatively impact their reputation and brand. As a
result, there are many more cases of data breaches suffered by organizations
than what is reported.
      Likewise, individuals are not legally required to report a breach of
their personal laptop. They are encouraged, however, to file a report with
the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) if they are victims of identity fraud.
A copy of that report, as well as one issued by the police, are frequently
required when dealing with merchants who may have issued credit under
the stolen identity. While the FTC received more than 650,000 reports of
identity theft in 2019, these reports are never released to the public.
      In addition to identity theft, social engineers will also target
individuals with ransomware. While criminals will demand much less
money from individuals compared to the ransom of enterprise data, it can
still cost the victim several thousand dollars. Another increasingly popular
extortion technique is demanding payment in exchange for not publicly
releasing compromising photos or sexually explicit emails secured from a
hack. While many cases of “sextortion” schemes are ultimately hoaxes or
empty threats, victims are terrified and frequently pay to avoid reputational
or legal problems.
     Assess Available Information
     The very first step an individual should take is to assess what
information they have already placed on social media and the measures
taken to safeguard it. Have they posted details such as their full date of
birth, upcoming vacation plans, travel itinerary or identifier information
regarding their children? If so, this is extremely exploitable and should be
removed. While it is unrealistic to recommend that people not use social
media, the risk of potential exploitation will be reduced if proper privacy
settings are put into place. Individuals must review the privacy settings for
all their social media accounts on a recurring basis. When privacy settings
are not restricted, social media users are opening themselves to the world.
The less information publicly available about a person, the less likely they
will become an actual target. Most public information on an individual is
shared by the individual themselves on their published profile and postings
on social media, giving social engineers considerable control.
      Individuals must also consistently evaluate the provision of
information to those who ask for it. The internet presents frequent
opportunities for individuals to obtain free products or services in exchange
for filling out a registration form requesting personal information. In such
cases, it is important to verify that the organization requesting such
information is legitimate. One of the oldest techniques for collecting
information over the internet is enticing an individual to provide
information during a registration process for a chance to win a prize. Unless
you can verify the legitimacy of the organization behind the event,
providing such information is very risky.
      Maintaining control over your information should be done in all facets
of your day-to-day life. If you are filling out background information at the
doctor’s office or for another service provider and they are asking for your
social security number, stop and ask them why they need it—in most cases,
they don’t. Don’t automatically provide sensitive personal data because
there is a line for it on the form.
    Carefully Screen Messages
    In addition to measures taken to safeguard personal information,
individuals must be vigilant in how they respond to incoming emails, text
messages, telephone calls and even knocks at their front door, as these
approaches can be used by social engineers to compromise security.
      Today, most of us carry mobile devices to check our email, search the
web, engage in social media platforms and make calls. Many of us use the
same devices to gain access to our office’s network server. As a result,
criminals are increasingly targeting personal mobile devices to gain access
to an organization’s sensitive data. Even if an individual believes they have
nothing personally worth stealing, they should think again—they can
become a conduit to their employer.
      Mobile devices are especially vulnerable when using public or
unprotected WiFi hotspots. If you are logging into sensitive accounts such
as your office server, bank account or healthcare portal while on these
hotspots, you are taking a big risk—your usernames and passwords can
easily become compromised. Be especially wary of free internet service
provided at places such as airports, restaurants or hotels. Anyone can set up
free WiFi and assign it a naming convention that closely resembles the
name of the establishment.
      Hackers love to obtain the usernames and passwords used by a target
to log into their personal accounts. A typical individual likely logs in to
more than 25 different online accounts on a weekly basis, making it
difficult to have—and remember—a unique username and password for
each account login. Most people will create one username and password
and use them across multiple platforms, but the hacker only needs to
discover one username and password to log in to most accounts. It is
important to use unique usernames and passwords for each login platform,
and there are a variety of password managing services available to help
keep track of them.
      When it comes to unsolicited telephone calls, callers can spoof the
number from which they are calling and pretend to be whoever they want to
be. They will use a variety of persuasive techniques such as authority by
claiming to be calling from the IRS or law enforcement. They may use
scarcity and claim that it is a limited time offer. They might even use social
consensus, asking you to make a donation to the fallen veterans.
      The best tactic for this threat is to not answer any number you are not
already familiar with. Let the call go to voicemail and then listen to the
voicemail. Most social engineers, including telemarketers, will not leave a
voicemail message—they prefer to call back and try to get you on the line,
where they can effectively try to manipulate you. If you do happen to speak
with someone by telephone who is trying to get you to buy a product or
provide information, do not comply. You can always ask for them to send
you the information or request via mail. If it is truly legitimate, they should
not have a problem in doing so.
    Navigate In-Person Interactions
    Many of us have received knocks on the door from vendors of products
and services including home security systems providers, painters, roofers or
landscapers. Did you reach out to them, or are they approaching you? You
cannot fully trust who they are or what they are saying. While being cordial,
take their information, but make no commitment. If you are interested, take
the time to verify their credentials. If they require a state license to offer
their services, go to the website of the licensing authority to see whether the
license exists and is in good standing. If they provide references on their
website or orally, do not trust these references—they can be staged. Look
for independent means to obtain a review.
      Despite my experience and understanding of social engineering
techniques, even I am not always immune to them, especially if they are
used by businesses, not malicious threat actors. Several months ago, I was
approached by someone inside of a major hardware store who wore a hat
with the logo of this store. He explained that they were conducting free
home energy audits and wanted to set up an appointment. Trusting the
brand name of the store, I decided to schedule the appointment. When
called to reconfirm the appointment, I was told that both my wife and I
needed to be present. This was an initial red flag for me—it sounded like it
might turn into a high-pressure sales attempt.
      The representative arrived right on time for the Saturday morning
appointment and went over several well-designed charts and graphs to draw
us into the mindset that a few minor upgrades could result in considerable
savings to our energy consumption and pocketbook. After conducting a 20-
minute survey of the home, he wrote up a proposal for three upgrades.
While attempting to show how these improvements would pay for
themselves within 18 months, I was taken aback by the cost, which was
broken down into components and labor.
      While a bit skeptical, I agreed to sign based upon a clause in the
contract that gave us three business days to cancel. Following his departure
from our home, I immediately got on the internet to research and verify the
market price for each of the components. The quote detailed in the contract
for the exact same components was 250 percent higher. This infuriated me.
      The following Monday, I decided to physically visit the office of this
contractor instead of attempting to cancel by phone. As I’m a certified fraud
examiner, I decided to wear my jacket with the Association of Certified
Fraud Examiners (ACFE) logo on it. Upon arrival, I asked to see the
general manager. I was led past a room of approximately 15 employees who
appeared to be working the telephones. I told the manager that I wanted to
cancel the contract because I was very uncomfortable with its lack of
transparency. Without asking me for further detail or trying to convince me
to reconsider, he pulled the paperwork and officially canceled the order.
      I subsequently lodged a complaint with the hardware store. I was told
that they periodically allow contractors to offer services to store customers
if the contractor purchases a number of the components through the store.
My response was that they needed to better vet such contractors as this
incident seriously damaged their store’s brand reputation in my eyes.
      This incident only reassured me of the need to never fully trust
everything said by someone who approaches you. While not working for
the store, this contractor leveraged the appearance of a direct affiliation to
gain the confidence and trust of unassuming customers. While unethical, it
was not illegal—the contract was written in accordance with state law.
While I exercised my right to rescind the contract within three business
days, the majority of individuals who sign likely don’t realize that they paid
too much until after speaking with neighbors several weeks or months later.
By that time, it is too late.
      There have always existed people who try to leverage blind trust and
social engineering skills to accomplish their objectives to the detriment of
their victims. Today, more than ever, a variety of resources on the internet
can be used to help verify, or call into question, their legitimacy.
      You are the first and best line of defense in the protection of yourself,
your family and your organization. To defeat most social engineering
efforts, remember whenever you receive an unsolicited email, text,
telephone call or face-to-face approach to verify before trusting.

The Future of Human Hacking
        “Humans should be worried about the threat posed by artificial intelligence.”

                                                                                    —Bill Gates




What     might we expect in the months and years ahead regarding the
continued successful use of social engineering methodology by various
threat actors to breach the security of organizations in pursuit of protected
data? Unfortunately, unless there is a significant shift in the mindset of
security professionals to place greater emphasis on security awareness
training for all insider employees and contractors, this will be a persistent
and ever-increasing threat.
      Of more concern are several evolving factors that will likely lead to an
increase in magnitude of such breaches by both criminal groups and state-
sponsored intelligence agencies. The first has to do with economic factors.
I’m writing this book while in the midst of the COVID-19 pandemic, which
will likely lead to a worldwide economic recession. Shortly after economies
enter a significant economic downturn, there is always a sharp increase in
criminal activity. It hits at all levels, ranging from petty theft, employee
fraud and household burglaries to armed robbery and security breaches of
organizations to steal valuable data.
      As more and more employees and contractors face severe economic
hardships, there will be an increasing number of individuals who will be
susceptible to becoming malicious insiders. Some of these insiders will
have significant training and experience in IT and related technologies.
They will also understand the vulnerabilities posed by human interaction
with technology and know how this can be leveraged for personal financial
gain. While some may decide to take such action on their own, others will
be susceptible targets for recruitment by criminal groups.
      Even more serious is the potential use of big data by criminal groups
and foreign intelligence services for targeting purposes. The first significant
data breaches of organizations go back to 2005 and have steadily increased
year by year. Imagine the value in being able to harness, triangulate and
analyze data—especially personal data—stolen over the past 15 years.
When we consider the more recent OPM and Equifax breaches, this should
not be discarded as a farfetched scenario. It is well within the realm of
possibilities.
      As discussed in Chapter 1, the data stolen from OPM and Equifax has
not appeared for resale anywhere on the deep web. That would suggest that
it is being held, and potentially analyzed, for future use. The individuals
who could be targeted using this information will be extremely vulnerable.
Some of them will likely be key U.S. government employees with access to
classified information, or in positions to influence decisions of benefit to a
foreign power. Others will hold key positions in nongovernmental entities
of interest who could be leveraged to conduct additional data breaches or
influence decisions.
      Finally, it will be very interesting to see the continued evolution of
technology and its relationship with humans. While some people fear that
advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) will ultimately lead to our loss
in control, I believe that we need to look at it in another light. Technology
is, and will continue to be, a mere tool used by humans. It is neither moral
nor immoral—humans decide how technology will be used. Just as it can
benefit the selfish interests of a few to the detriment of society, it can
provide considerable benefits to all of mankind.
      While technology evolves, basic human psychology remains rather
constant. People will continue to have basic needs. They will also continue
to experience human emotions as they relate to their environment. Most
importantly, from a social engineer’s perspective, people will always have
motivations and vulnerabilities that can be manipulated.
      The perspective danger is how social engineers will harness
technology in the manipulation of their targets. Today it is clear—we can
directly see how social engineers benefit from using the internet to collect
data on organizations and individuals. Communication channels currently
used by social engineers such as email, SMS, phone calls (to include call
spoofing) and social media platforms are relatively new technological tools
that will continue to evolve or be replaced. In the future, AI might reduce or
eliminate a social engineer’s limitations and vulnerabilities.
      Today, social engineers are limited in the amount of targeting data we
can collect, and especially the amount of data that we can retain. As a
result, social engineers are unable to analyze information they glean during
conversations with the target in conjunction with all available information
about the topic or individual. We have limited intellectual brain power.
      Additionally, social engineers have our own emotions that can become
distractions and sometimes get in the way of our objectives. While we do
our best in trying to read the emotions of our targets, we are limited to
analyzing their verbal communication in conjunction with some nonverbal
cues.
      If social engineers are able to harness the use of evolving AI for
human manipulation, we can only speculate on the exponential threat.
Whether 25, 50 or 100 years from now, man will likely produce robots that
will be able to mimic human form. If so, their potential strengths could
include the ability to maximize all available data on targets, quickly analyze
new data provided by a target and instantaneously be able to formulate and
execute techniques to advance manipulation toward overall objectives. Such
technology might also have the ability to read human emotions through
verbal and nonverbal communication, including changes in breathing, eye
movement, blood pressure or voice.
      What about the potential weaknesses of robots? Perhaps there will be
none. While androids will learn how to express emotions such as sadness,
joy, compassion and empathy when interacting with a target, they will not
internalize them. As a result, they will not have any emotional
vulnerabilities. They will be exclusively focused on executing the plan of
their human taskmasters with extreme precision. As such, their human
targets will continue to remain the weakest link in an organization’s
security.
      Technology and the way social engineers use it will continue to evolve,
while humans—along with their motivations, vulnerability and instinct to
trust—will remain a constant. While organizations can’t manage the myriad
of AI-fueled schemes social engineers might use to manipulate a target in
the future, they can do their best to make sure their security posture’s
weakest link remains its first line of defense.

                                  Glossary
    Backstopping: Support obtained and used to add credence to a cover
story or legend. It may be material such as business cards, websites or
brick-and-mortar offices, or it can be a simple verification by someone
answering a telephone number.

    Business Email Compromise (BEC): This is an attack where the
perpetrator gains access to the IT network of a target organization and
electronically impersonates a senior official, providing fraudulent wire
transfer instructions to an employee for execution.

   Cover Story: Also referred to as a cover legend, it is a fictitious
account of who the person claims to be and the purpose of their activity.

    Dark Web: The portion of the internet within the deep web that is only
accessible through special software, allowing users to remain anonymous. It
is frequently used by the criminal world for the exchange of products,
services and information.

    Deep Web: The portion of the web that is not indexed and therefore not
discoverable by means of standard search engines.

    Elicitation: Methodology used by social engineers to extract sensitive
information during a casual conversation with an unwitting target.

   Human Hacking: Also referred to as social engineering, it is the art of
manipulating people to undertake an activity that meets the objective of the
hacker.

    Insider: An employee or contractor who has unescorted access within
an organization.

    Malicious Insider: An employee or contractor who decides to cause
financial or other harm to the organization.
   Open-Source Intelligence (OSINT): Information that is publicly
available.

    Phishing: A common social engineering technique using an email to
entice a recipient to click on a link or open an attachment, allowing for
malware to be downloaded.

   Pretext: Similar to a cover story, it is typically a false pretense used
while engaging someone over the telephone.

    Ransomware: Security breach attack by which the threat actor encrypts
the target’s data and holds it hostage until a ransom, usually in the form of
bitcoin, is paid.

    Smishing: Similar to phishing, it is a common social engineering
technique using a SMS text message to entice a recipient to click on a link
or open an attachment, allowing for malware to be downloaded.

   Social Engineering: Also referred to as human hacking, it is the art of
manipulating people to undertake an activity that meets the objective of the
hacker.

   Spear Phishing: An email that has been carefully crafted to target a
specific individual.

    State Actor: Someone conducting intelligence activities under the
direction or interest of a nation state.

    Surface Web: The portion of the internet that is indexed and accessible
by standard search engines.

    Vishing: The use of a telephone by a social engineer to gather
information from a target or manipulate them to undertake a certain action.
