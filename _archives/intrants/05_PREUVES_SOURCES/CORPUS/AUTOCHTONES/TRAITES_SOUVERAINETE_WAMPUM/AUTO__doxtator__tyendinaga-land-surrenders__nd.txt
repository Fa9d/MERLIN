                    INTRODUCTION

The first half of the nineteenth century, perhaps more
than any other period, helped to shape the particular develop-
ment of Ontario native communities. In 1775.both the British
and the Americans had anxiously sought the military support of
the Iroquois and other native peoples."1 By 1830 the frontier
between the United States and British North America had become
more stable and the value of Indian military efforts began to
be questioned. Increasingly, the Indian nations were viewed
not as potential allies whose friendship and favour were to be
maintained at all costs, but as dependent children who required
the instruction and assistance of missionaries and administrators
to carry out their affairs. Of even greater significance to
to the way of life of Indian peoples was the enactment of the
government policy of extracting from Indians large tracts of
land to accommodate the ever increasing numbers.of white settlers.
With the loss of land came the loss of economic independence, as
the old traditional economy of hunting, fishing and horticulture
rapidly became an impossibility.
The settlement of Mohawks at Tyendinaga was one native
community which was particularly affected by pressure from

Historical Background to the Tyendinaga Settlement

Prior to the American Revolution, the Tyendinaga Mohawks
had lived at Fort Hunter on the Mohawk River m New York.1
Canajoharie, the other principal settlement of the Mohawks in
New York, was approximately thirty-six miles north of Fort
Hunter. These two communities together with a few families at
Schoharie made up the Mohawk component in the Iroquois League
of the Six Nations, a loose confederation of the Mohawk, Oneida
Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and Tuscarora nations.4 The traditional
territories of the Iroquois were principally located in north
central New York. The Huron, an Iroquoian-speaking confederacy
closely related to the New York Iroquois, occupied northwestern
sections of southern Ontario until their dispersal in 1650, After
this date the Iroquois used the north shore of Lake Ontario exten-. .:
sively for satellite fishing and hunting villages until the end
of the seventeenth century (see following page). Historical accounts

village was loosely supervised by an hereditary council composed

political leader was not characteristic of this 1820-1840 period.

perceived by the Mohawks to be held in common by the tribe.^

The settlement pattern of the nineteenth century Mohawks
also reflected certain characteristics of their government.      The
geographical separation of the Mohawks into two areas of settle-

The lower settlement was comprised of Mohawk supporters of certain
peace chiefs. Another faction, led by John Loft, a Stockbridge
Indian who had been active in the War of 1812, lived the furthest
away from the water.1   The Loft party was joined in its opposit-

      During the years 1820 to 1840, the British government's
methods of dealing with Indian peoples changed drastically.
Although throughout the period the government continued to be
intellectually committed to the idea of protecting the Indian,
that protection after the late 1820 s became a highly selective
one directed towards altering the Indian's values and subsistence
system.   There are many reasons why such a change took place—
the decline in the military importance of Indians, the rise of
philanthropic religious organizations, the adoption of liberal
ideas by the British government1-- but what is perhaps less
readily seen is the role that the problem of land management in
Upper Canada played in the colonial government's application
of the new ideas of philanthropy and moral reclamation.   In 1820
when the need for land for settlement was relatively low, the
government's method of obtaining a surrender from the Tyendinaga
Mohawks was through the established traditional channels of
negotiation.   By I835 when immigration had more than doubled the
population of Upper Canada since 18202 the land needs of the
British government had changed.   The increasing immigration that
was not to reach its peak until the 1840 s and 185c s3 created
the need for the rapid opening up of large new territories. The
Indian department during this period managed to adapt the rise
of a new humanitarian philosophy to their new needs for land.

It was believed that Indians should be protected from white

white communities was to persuade the Indians to surrender
a portion of their lands for settlers and to collect the Indians
on a separate parcel of land.    The I835 surrender took place

gave the government both the means and the moral justification
for demanding the required large surrenders of land from Indian
peoples.
      By the late 1830$   it was clear that the Mohawk's surrender
of the northern block of land was not enough to supply the demand
for well-situated land with potential for economic development.
The Indians would have to permit the settlement of at least part
of the unsurrendered back tract and front tract to settlers,
lumberers and mill entrepreneurs.   By this time the initial
optimism about the sweeping success of the civilization program
had faded as the Indians' progress appeared to be very slow..
The reason for the lack of success of the civilization program^
the government suggested, was the fact that the Indians should
not be separated from the good examples of white settlers.
It was now deemed to be in the Mohawks' best interests to allow
white settlers and businessmen to farm, cut timber, and build mills
in close proximity, leasing nearby land as models for the Mohawks
to follow. The Indian department did not manufacture the policy
of civilization merely to obtain Indian land, but it did adapt
the policy and philosophy of missionary societies to meet the
government's particular land requirements.
