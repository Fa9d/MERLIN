WHAT IS SPECIAL ABOUT HANDBOOK OF THE AINU LANGUAGE?

   Not the first English description of Ainu!
    The first full-scale Ainu-English-Japanese dictionary with an attached short grammar was
    published by the Anglican Church missionary Rev. John Batchelor in 1887.

   This is the first comprehensive description of Ainu in English., i.e., covering many different aspects in
    many Ainu dialects:

        Part I: Overview of Ainu studies
         (Ainu ethnogenesis, history of Ainu studies, history of Ainu, language contact, dialects, oral literature,
         revitalization)

        Part II: Typologically interesting characteristics of the Ainu language
         (vowel co-occurrence restrictions, polysynthesis, combinability of various voice markers, noun
         incorporation, tripartite alignment, nominal classification distinguishing ordinary and locative nouns,
         elaborate possessive classes, verbal number, a rich four-term evidential system, and
         undergrammaticalized aspect)

 AINU ORAL LITERATURE
 In the early 20th century, severe ethnic and linguistic oppression by the Japanese state
  resulted in the rapid abandonment of the Ainu language.
 Not used as a language of daily communication since the 1950s.

 But, for half a century Ainu was remembered by older speakers who used it for
  transmitting Ainu oral literature.
 Sophisticated oral literatures, at least 3 genres: melodic yukar ‘heroic epics’ and
  kamuy yukar ‘songs of gods’, and prose uwepeker ‘folktales’ (Endō 2022, Chapter 10).
 See Nakagawa, Hiroshi, Anna Bugaeva, Miki Kobayashi, and Yoshimi Yoshikawa. 2016–
  2021. A glossed audio corpus of Ainu folklore. Tokyo: National Institute for Japanese
  Language and Linguistics. Available online at:
  https://ainu.ninjal.ac.jp/folklore/en/

THE SPREAD OF AINU
   The predecessor of Ainu arrived to Japan from southern East Asia 30,000 years ago (Stanyon
    et al. 2009), when the Japanese islands were connected to the mainland.
   Then at the beginning of the modern interglacial period c. 10,000 years ago, sea level began
    to rise, and for many years the ancient population of Japan was left in isolation.
   3,000 years ago wet rice agriculturalists, who were speakers of Japanese, started moving to
    western Kyūshū in Japan from the southwest of the Korean Peninsula (the future kingdom of
    Paekche) (Janhunen 2023: 141).
   The migration to Japan continued throughout the Yayoi (900/300 BCE to 300 CE) and Kofun
    (300–500 CE) periods resulting in the replacement of all multiple lineages that had evolved in
    the Neolithic Jōmon Japan, except for the Ainuic language family.
   Ainu spread to Hokkaido by c. 1000 CE , to southern Sakhalin by c. 1300 CE and to the
    Kuril Islands by c. 1500–1600 CE.
   Ainu too could have contributed to the reduction of linguistic diversity on the Japanese
    archipelago by sweeping away or absorbing other Jōmon languages on its northward spread
    to Hokkaido (Janhunen 2022: 63, 65, Chapter 2).

WHY IS AINU SO IMPORTANT?
   Ainu is head-marking, polysynthetic and incorporating.

   Most Northeast Asian languages are strikingly different from Ainu.

   Ainu is likely to be much more typical of what languages were like in Northeast Asia
    several millennia ago than the picture we would get from Japanese or Korean.

NUMBER AS A GRAMMATICAL CATEGORY:VERBAL NUMBER IN AINU
  •   Ainu has verbal number (a singular vs. plural distinction in verbs) instead of
      nominal number as in English.

  •   In case of intransitive verbs, verbal number refers to the number of subject
      participants:

         terkeype ahu-n ‘a frog jumped’
         terkeype ahu-p ‘(many) frogs jumped’

  •   In case of transitive verbs, verbal number refers to the number of object participants
      or event number (number of actions):

         cep tuy-e ‘cut (one) fish’‐ cep tuy-pa ‘cut (many) fish, cut (one) fish several times’
         yuk rayke ‘kill (one) deer’ ‐ yuk ronnu ‘kill (many) deer’
         Cf. English kill vs. massacre 'kill many people in a short period of time’.

 MARKING EVIDENTIALITY IN AINU
  Evidentiality: the indication of the nature of evidence for a given statement.
  (Seen? Felt? Heard? Inferred? Other?)
 Ainu has a four-term evidential system consisting of the following markers, all
  originating in nouns, which is rather unusual crosslinguistically
 (Takahashi 2022, Chapter 18):

    •   Visual: sir lit. ‘appearance’
    •   Non-visual sensory: hum lit. ‘sound’
    •   Hearsay: (h)aw lit. ‘voice’
    •   Inferential: ru lit. ‘footprint’

        (Tokachi: eastern Hokkaido Ainu)

SUMMARY

   This volume will open the field of Ainu studies to the world and help to attract many new students
    and researchers.


   Being so different from other Northeast Asian languages, Ainu can definitely maximize the accuracy
    of our view of earlier linguistic ecologies in the region’s deep past.


   Ainu will help people to appreciate the linguistic and cultural contribution of the Ainu of Japan to
    human intellectual heritage.


   This volume will make a step forward in the process of the so-called “gradual rediscovery of Japan as
    an ethnically and culturally diverse nation” (Morris-Suzuki 1998: 183).
