                   Introduction
                         by Bill McKibben

Though the three expeditions chronicled in this overpowering book
took place almost exactly a century ago, much in them will be famil-
iar to the reader who has just put down,, say, Into Thin Air, Jon
Krakauer's epic account of a season on Mt. Everest.
       Take, for instance, the way the Hubbard-Wallace treks were in
effect commercialized, media-driven crusades. True, they lacked
satellite phones to keep in touch with their sponsors, and there was
no cross-labrator.org to check for daily updates. But Leonidas
Hubbard, Jr. was a junior editor of Outing magazine, and he
dreamed up his trip to the hunting grounds of the Naskapi Indians
as a literary adventure from the very start. Labrador had just
enough appeal (the largest unexplored wilderness remaining in
North America) that he was able to get an assignment from
Outings editor, Caspar Whitney. Still, Whitney, who had snow-
shoed two thousand miles across the Northwest Territories in 1895,
doubted whether the Naskapi trek, taking place in mid-summer,
was really in the magazine's league. And his dubiousness infected
his young writer, who knew he needed to "make his name" with
this trip; at every juncture when turning back would have saved his
life, he reflected that he did not yet have enough material to make a
story. Just like the Everest guides, who knew business would suffer
if they did not ferry their clients to the summit, Hubbard realized
that coming back empty-handed would cost him dearly. Each time
these expeditions returned to New York, a crowd of newspapermen
would be waiting at the pier, and those omnivorous journalistic
hordes did as much to shape these travels as the omnivorous clouds
of mosquitoes and blackflies.
       And consider the way that death spices the tale. When
Leonidas Hubbard dies of starvation in the forced march back
towards his base camp, what had been merely a story of flybitten
struggle and poor navigation becomes an epic, with at least four
gospel accounts, plenty to set people arguing. Then as now, a fasci-
x                            Introduction

nation with the particulars of dying haunts most great exploration
stories - it's the only way to prove you were really taking a big
enough risk to be worth celebrating. When we stop to think about
it, we realize that simply training to the point where you can climb
Mt. Everest takes great force of will, and that in fact the very best
and wisest climbers are the ones least likely to make the mistakes of
judgment that kill them - but if you hike right up and hike right
down, you've not got a story. If you want immortality, mortality is
a prerequisite.
       So, if there's so much that's familiar about these tales, why
have they survived for a century? The answer, of course, is that they
haven't. Dillon Wallace's account of the original trek and Hubbard's
death sold pretty well, launching him on a career as an outdoors
writer. When he and Hubbard's widow, Mina, returned two
summers later on competing expeditions to retrace the original
journey, they each produced books that did quite well, and Mina
lectured widely. But in the course of a few decades the books went
out of print, and then the various diaries disappeared into far-flung
archives or into the bottom of forgotten attic trunks, there to be
rediscovered in the latter part of the century by John Rugge and Jim
Davidson.
       It is what they have done with those brittle documents that
makes Great Heart one of the few truly compelling pieces of explo-
ration writing that I know of.
       To understand their achievement, you need to know that
doing history - or literary writing of any kind - is in certain ways
quite akin to exploring. Unless you're writing something formulaic
and obvious, you set your sights on the unknown. By definition,
there are very few maps, and those sketchy. You make many a
wrong turn, head up may a wrong creek, waste many a day in fruit-
less scouting. But if you are well prepared, hard working, and lucky,
the day will come when you will begin to understand the terrain
you are exploring. Your characters will gradually start to come
alive; you will understand who they are in three dimensions. It is
easy to do that badly, of course - to impose your own view of the
world on some helpless historical figure long in the grave. But the
reader can tell when that happens.
       And the reader can tell, as in this story, when the writer has
truly done his work well - when people spring willy-nilly to life.
       Partly it is a matter of context. Davidson and Rugge have
spent so much time canoeing these waters that they know what the
                             Introduction                            xi



real obstacles must have been: the inscrutable, unbearable insects;
the tight spruce groves; the rapids too big to run safely but too small
to absolutely demand an energy-sapping portage.
        But even more, such storytelling depends on strong, generous
imagining - on figuring out what it is that makes people in a distant
time do the things they do. It is no accident, for instance, that at
many moments in these treks people comfort themselves by
recourse to the Bible. A psalm before bed, a hymn around the
campfire. To our ears it would sound pious and odd, but these men
and women lived by a different code. If we were about to be aban-
doned and to starve to death in a tent in the Labrador wilds, we
might not choose to have our departing companion read us
Corinthians 13: "If I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but
have not love, I am nothing." But that Hubbard so chose helps
explain his undaunted courage unto the end, and the devotion of his
wife that drives the second half of this book.
        The greatest of the imaginings - because it has the least to go
on - concerns George Elson, the mixed-blood guide who goes with
Hubbard and Wallace on their first trek and then leads Mrs.
Hubbard's subsequent venture. Elson's diary is pretty thin - how
many fish caught, how many miles covered - but in its silences
Rugge and Davidson manage to catch the undeniable hint of his
blossoming affection for Mina Hubbard. It is a falling-in-love story,
as he sees her indomitable spirit and also sees the sweet girlishness
that might not have been too common in the hardscrabble outposts
where he had grown up. Teaching her to shoot, and then to speak a
little Cree by firelight, hauling her down from dangerous rocks,
racing with her hand-in-hand from a caribou charge - there's a
Technicolor, halo-ed quality to this summer trip, a moment of
immense freedom from conventional roles for both of them. And so
much the sadder, then, when with the end of the trip in sight Mina
snaps back into New England primness, even making her erstwhile
and stumble-tongued swain sign a declaration that nothing "unbe-
coming honorable Christian men and women" had happened
between them. In fact, the sadness at the end requires the greatest
imagining for us, because we are not as tightly bound by gender and
class as once folk were; in a modern version, they would of course
follow their bliss and all would end happily.

 is fully satisfying but by no means unique. There is plenty of canoe-
