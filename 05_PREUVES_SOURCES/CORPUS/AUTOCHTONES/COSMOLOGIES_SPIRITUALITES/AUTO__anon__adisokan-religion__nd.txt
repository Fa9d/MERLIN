tales of the people
an introduction

     The anishinabe people did not have
 a written history. The people of the
 woodland relumed the human unity
 of tribal life through visual memories
 and personal dreams and songs and
 tales.
   The tales in this book about the
origin and religion of the anishinabe
people and the birth and life of
manabozho — the        compassionate
trickster of the anishinabe spirit—
have been told many times in many
different ways by many different
people.
  This book    is another voice of the
many tales the peop/e told during the
long winter nights in the woodland
when they were sure manabozho was
not about listening in the face of an
animal or flower.
  The tales of the anishinabe are not
an objective collection and interpreta-
tion of facts. Stories are a circle of
dreams and oratorical gestures show-
ing the meaning between the present
and the past in the life of the tribal
people of the woodland.
   The anishinabe believe in the past
because people are the center of the
tales — the people feel the past in the
present   — which change as they are
told again and again just as the sun
and the moon and the people change.
   Many modern listeners would like
to hear the original authorized histori-
cal and cultural legends of the anish-
inabe    past for easy memory       and
analysis. But the stories of the past
only show the humor and compassion
of the present.
   The tales in this book were first
printed almost a century ago in The
Progress    — a weekly newspaper pub-
lished on the White Earth indian
reservation in Minnesota.
   The first issue of The Progress was
distributed on March 25, 1886 —
twenty years after the White Earth
reservation was organized to contain
the many families of the anishinabe
people living in different parts of the
state.
    The tales edited and published in
 this book were originally published in
an eleven-part series in 7he Progress
 between    December 1887 and May
 1888. The insertion of certain anish-
inabe words and punctuation and
grammatical corrections are the only
major editorial changes in the stories
from the original publication.
    The insertion of anishinabe words is
intended to give the listener a feeling
for the language of the anishinabe
people — a language which the- domi-
nant society once would not allow the
people to speak.
  Theodore   H. Beaulieu, the editor of
The Progress, wrote in the original
introduction to the series published in
the newspaper that two members of
the midewiwin — Day Dodge and Say-
cosegay — told the meaning of the
religious life of the people in the
anishinabe \anguage which was then
translated by Beaulieu for publication.
The editor and publisher of The
Progress were     both of anishinabe
ancestry and members of the tribe.
   When the section on the midewiwin
was first published in The Progress
Day Dodge was said to be over ninety
years of age, which means that he was
born before the turn of the nineteenth
century when the peop/e were not yet
colonized and suppressed on indian
reservations.
   The edited tales in this book about
the compassionate trickster manaboz-
ho were written and published under
the name of Theodore H. Beaulieu —
the editor of The Progress.
  The   illustrations   in this book   are
enlarged photographic reproductions
of the original anishinabe pictomyths
first published about the turn of the
last century by the Bureau of Ameri-
can Ethnology in two books: Chip-
pewa Music by Frances Densmore and
The Midewiwin or Grand Medicine
Society of the Ojibway by W. J.
Hoffman.
   Words in the anishinabe language
have been italicized for emphasis. The
phonetic spelling of anishinabe words
 — the language of the people was not
written — within the stories conforms
with the entries transcribed      in A
Dictionary of the Otchipwe Language
by Bishop Baraga. The word defini-
tions which appear at the end of this
book also follow the Baraga Diction-
ary which was first published about a
century ago when the tales were first
printed in The Progress.
   The    italicism   of certain   other
words   — such as /ndian and reserva-
tion — is intended to emphasize eth-
nocentric     semantic   blunders.  The
name anishinabe has been consistantly
used in this book rathern than chip-
pewa or ojibway indians which are all
invented names for the people.
   The word anishinabe means the
people in the language of the anish-
inabe people. The word adisokan in
the title of this book means a tale or
story.

   You have asked me to tell you the
customs of our ancestors and the origin
of the anishinabe. My nojishe, it is your
wish and I shall tell you our beliefs.
   The first appearance of our people, the
anishinabe, occurred in this way:
   When the kitchimanito made up his
mind   to create   man,   he took a handful
 of earth and rubbed it together in his
 palms and a man was formed. The manito
 below the earth, who was a very imposing
 manito, with heavy locks of white hair,
 said to kitchimanito in counsel: What are
you going to do with only one anishinabe?
 In answer to this question, kitchimanito
 took another handful of earth and rubbed
 it in his palms and a woman was formed.
Then he said, this person shall be the
fruit of the earth, and the seed from which
shall come the anishinabe people. This my
nojishe, is how the anishinabe originated
and became so numerous.

   I shall now tell you our customs re-
garding the naming of children, and the
customs by which their lives are guided.
It is believed that every child, while he is
in   his   mother’s   womb,   wonders   what
disposition his parents are going to make
of him. When a child is about to be born,
his parents begin accumulating provisions
and clothing. After his birth his parents
hold a feast.
   A spokesman is selected by the father
who is supposed to act for the child. A
meeting of some of the principal men of
the tribe is held and it is decided who
shall be the niiawee — the person to whom
the life of the child will be dedicated. It
does not always follow that the child will
be named after the niiawee but he must
in the course of time give the child some
name.
   When the selection of a niiawee has
been made, the spokesman who has been
24




selected to preside over the meeting, in-
vokes the kitchimanito to guard the future
of the anishinabe child and give him a
long life.
   A pipe with a long decorated stem is
lighted, and offered as a sacrifice to the
kitchimanito in this way: the stem is first
pointed in the direction of the waban,
the jawan, the ningabian, the giwedin and
then downwards towards the earth. After
this ceremony is concluded, the person
who has been selected to name the child
is called in and informed of the duty
imposed upon him.
   A nickname is generally given by the
parents or some other relative of the
child. The real name of the anishinabe
child is given only by his niiawee, which
is usually done when the child has become
sick for the first time.
   My nojishe, it was the custom of our
ancestors to be guided throughout their
lives by their dreams... the most im-
portant dreams guided our ancestors. Dur-
ing his life, an anishinabe dreams about
many animals and birds and some one of
these impress him deeply. The person who
                                          25




is selected to name a child, tells the child
of some dream about a certain animal
and says, niiawee will be named after it,
andI will go after the animal which I have
told you about.
   His main object then is to procure the
animal he may have dreamed of, which
the kitchimanito usually allowed him to
secure. The animal was cooked and served
out at a grand feast for friends and rela-
tives. The niiawee then commences a
chant, which has been composed by him
for the occasion,   and of words which he
has dreamed of at sometime during his
life.
   The animal which has been cooked is
a peace offering to the kitchimanito by
the person who named the child, because
he dreamed of this and knew how to
conciliate the kitchimanito who permitted
the anishinabe oshki abinodji to be born
and allowed him to live. The kitchimanito
very seldom takes the child away because
he pities the anishinabe who have confi-
dence in him. This my nojishe, was the
way our anishinabe ancestors named their
children.

   Bakan ishkotawe was a custom followed
by anishinabe women, and was established
among them by manabozho of whom I
will have much to say later. It will take
a long time to tell about him as all our
traditions refer to him. When the earth
was covered with water he was the being
who formed a new earth. But I am going
to speak now of the custom regarding
women.
   My nojishe, you see by the locks of my
hair which have been whitened by the
snows of many winters that I am a very
aged man, and many, many moons ago
the customs which I intend to speak
about, were followed by anishinabe wo-
men. Since the old men of our tribe have
all dropped away and disappeared from
the earth, you never hear that a young
girl who has just reached puberty is re-
moved or sent away from a village during
the time when nature is changing her from
30




childhood to womanhood. Neither do you
ever hear of bakan ishkotawe for a woman
now. But in those past days our women
had strong restrictions placed over them.
   When a young girl first became aware
of the change nature was about to make
with her, she immediately left the village
to which she belonged and built at some
distance away from it a small lodge ofa
sufficient size to allow her to lie down
comfortably, but not high enough to stand
in it. She remained in the bakan ishkotawe
so long as she could fast, which was from
five to ten days. She would not go any
distance from her lodge, for if a man
crossed her tracks while she was under-
going her transition, he immediately fell
to the ground paralyzed and it became

called in to cure him.
   My nojishe, you cannot properly under-
stand without an illustration, how strong
the mino manito and matchi manito are
during this period.
   When I was a young man I had many
warts on my hands...I          was almost

   When a young man reached manhood
he selected the longest days in the year,
which was generally late sigwang and
wandered away from the village into the
lonely forest and where he would proceed
to build a wasisswan in some tree and in
which he would lie down and commence
his giigwishimowin, which usually lasted
from five to ten days without food or
water. During the fast he had many
dreams, and by those dreams the course
of his future life was guided, both on the
war path and in hunting.

   A young man once wandered from his
tribe in sigwang time to undergo the
customary giigwishimowin. After he had
fasted for several days his father came to

  My nojishe, you want to know            now
about our marriage customs. Well,         very
many moons ago when my people            wore
no dress but that made from the           furs
and   skins   of animals   slain in the chase,
and there were many        very handsome wo-
 men and men. A young man would say,
Iam good looking, I don’t think I could
find a woman to suit me; however, he
would soon see some young woman who
would impress him very much. He would
then go on a hunt and select some fine
furs and skins, which he would carry to
the wigiwam of the young girl to make
her dresses to adorn her person, and if
she accepted his attentions she would in
return make him something whereby to
adorn his person also, which was gen-
erally a handsome pair of makisin. They
were then betrothed to each other, and
after this if everything was satisfactory
with the parents of the young anishinabe
50




woman, she would then go in the neighbor-
hood of her intended husband’s home
and cut mitig which she could carry on
her back and take it to the wigiwam of
his parents. The wood was deposited at
the entrance outside where she would
await further developments. If there were
no   objections,   his   mother   would   come
out and in a gentle manner would proceed
to pinch or blow the nose of the young
woman as a token that she was welcome
as one of the family. Then, after the lapse
of ten days from this time, the young
couple would again meet together, and
the man would take the hand of his bride
and say we must live for one another,
we must be true and live together until
we die. They were then looked upon as
man and wife.
   Very often our men would take two
and three wives and mostly of the same
family, that is, sisters, as there were
fewer family quarrels in this way. The
best hunters always had the most wives.
When a married man would see a woman
whom he would fancy, he would pay his
respects to her and if his attentions were

favorably received, he would then consult
the judgment of the other members of
his family, and if there arose no objections
the new applicant was brought home to
the bosom of his family. If there were
objections,   the   new   bride would   remain
with her parents or an additional wigiwam
would be added to the much married
possessions of the man. The wife which
was first married generally ranks first in
the family although the children of the
different wives were looked upon with
equal consideration by the father.
  Another custom among my people and
which many of them practice to this day
is, when a man falls in love and would
marry some young woman, he takes his
blanket and goes in the neighborhood of
her home,     and awaits when    all have re-
tired for the night and the camp fires
have burned low, when he proceeds to
enter the wigiwam and having assured
himself where the young woman is re-
posing, which is generally by the side
of her mother, he quietly lies down beside
the young woman and if she accepts of
his courtship, they proceed to chat quietly

during the night — the lover taking his

as a last resort, the mother of the young

   My nojishe, you ask me if there was
any jealousy in those days. Well, it was
as it is today, only the spite was perhaps
more bitter and the: revengeful feeling

to disfigure the face and the latter to
disgrace the victim. Among the men death

affairs.

   It may not be known to many of our
readers that not many years ago there
existed a class of individuals among the
Indians who were known as tchissakiwinini,
whose vocations and feats were similar to
those of the eastern jugglers in such feats
as the rope-tieing, knife-swallowing and
fire-eating tricks. These beings were looked
upon as equal to the mashkikiwinini in
many respects, in fact we may say that
the representatives of the healing art were
divided into four classes: Mashkikiwinini,
dreamers,   blowers,   and _ tchissakiwinini.
   We will now speak of the tchissakiwinini.
They were supposed to be leagued with
the imaginary dwellers of the manito akki
and to hold communion with the differ-
ent manitog who presided over the destiny
of the dead and living worlds. Prominent
among which was the mechekans — king
of the turtles — who was regarded as a
very powerful manito of good or evil, and
58




who could converse with and look all
over the world. Of course there were many
other manito of minor power, but were
all subject to the turtle. And the mechekans
would communicate with the outside world
through the tchissakiwinini, and when
any person desired to know something
about absent friends or relatives, or
whether a sick person would overcome
sickness and live, or whether death would
follow, and where one would find some
lost and stolen articles, as also a medium
between the living and the dead to bring
tidings from departed friends. they would
repair to some fchissakiwinini and con-
sult him about any subject they wished.
   The mode of proceeding was generally
in this wise: when anyone wished some
favor of the mechekans, they would go
and consult the ¢chissakiwinini taking
some gift along, usually assema with which
to please the manito and be deserving of
the good will of all the other manitog
who may take part in the ceremony. If
everything was favorable, the inquiring
party would    be informed   and invited to
come   at a stated time and place, where

the tchissakiwinini would have erected a
tchissakan, which was built of stout poles
eight to ten feet long driven firmly into
the ground,    and   in such a manner       as to
form a circular body. These were attached

tales of the anishingbe people
edited with anishinabe words by
gerald vizenor

The tales in this book have been told many times by
tie anishinabe people of the woodland.   The tales of
the people were first collected and printed almost 2
century ago in a newspaper published on the
anishinabe white earth reservation. The anishinabe
pictomyths were first published by the Bureau of
American Ethnology. The anishinabe are known in
the dominant society by the invented names ojibway
and chippewa. In the language of the people the
anishinabe are the woodland people.
