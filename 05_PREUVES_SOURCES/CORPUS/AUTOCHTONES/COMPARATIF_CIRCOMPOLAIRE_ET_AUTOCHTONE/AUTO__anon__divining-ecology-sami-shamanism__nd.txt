             DIVINING ECOLOGY: THE SÁMI SHAMAN DRUM


                                     ABSTRACT


       Humans have long sought knowledge of the future, and in their seeking

have turned to rocks, sticks, stars, and even the body for answers. Divination is

one of humanity’s oldest spiritual practices and its cultural significance reveals

much about the lived experience of people and the places they inhabit. Whether a

burning bush, an auspicious star, or a symbolic dream, the tools, rituals, and

practices of divination are necessarily rooted in the natural world but invite

participation in the supernatural.

       The Sámi people are an indigenous community who have lived in the

circumpolar region of Eurasia for millennia. Before forced Christianization, Sámi

culture was anchored in a shamanic worldview that was deeply connected to and

influenced by the natural environment of the Arctic. One especially unique feature

of this pre-Christian tradition was the Sámi shaman drum—a sacred tool used for

ritual, music, and most importantly, divination.

       This dissertation examines the relationship between divination and

ecology in the context of the Sámi shaman drum. Drawing upon methodological

approaches from religious studies and the philosophy of religion, the research

draws attention to the way certain dominant epistemologies and methodologies

limit our understanding of divination and other forms of traditional ecological


                                         iv
knowledge. By analyzing the historical context and symbolic language of the

drum of Sámi noaidi Anders Poulsen (c.1600–1692), this dissertation

demonstrates the benefits of utilizing indigenous research methods for deeper

understanding of embedded and embodied knowledges.

        The findings of this research show that the divinatory use of the Sámi

drum supports its function as an ecological and cosmological symbol within Sámi

culture. Additionally, the methods used provide a template for further

examinations into the ways cultural divination practices reflect a spiritual

relationship to the natural world. Finally, these results suggest that by imbuing

nature with the capacity to generate and reveal knowledge, divination is more

than just the solicitation of answers; it is also a spiritual practice to make divine

the natural world.

                                 Prologue: Sápmi

       If you tried to find Sápmi on a map, you couldn't. That fact summarizes

the personal, social, academic, and spiritual significance of this dissertation. There

is power in names and language, and when an aggressive, dominant culture

expands into the territory of indigenous lands, one of the easiest ways to posture

that dominance (aside from the oft-used method of brutal genocide) is to rename

the valleys, rivers, and mountains; to call the land by new words making those

who lived there before feel foreign in their own home.

       My journey to find Sápmi, both literally and metaphorically, was the result

of a series of insights, interpretations, and passionate longings that were ignited

by a trip to Iceland. In the summer of 2016, I spent one week traveling around this

small, dynamic island with my family. Iceland is a breathtaking country with

great spaces between its small towns. It is, in the summer, bright and green with

nearly twenty-four hours of daylight, so many waterfalls that at some point you

cease to be amazed by their appearance on a rocky mountainside, and yet an

austerity of place that evokes the strength of those very few humans who have

called the island home. As a passenger crossing the expansive lands one cannot

help but be hypnotized by the landscape as it slowly unfolds beyond the car

window.

       After hours of this hypnotic gazing, I started to notice a particular

landscape feature that appeared at seemingly regular intervals and was definitely

natural, but also appeared to be man-made. These environmental features were

stone stacks that measured roughly five feet in diameter by eight feet in height,


                                          2
and the only thing I could surmise from their appearance near the roadside was

that perhaps they acted as guideposts during the brutal Icelandic winters that

transformed the lush green landscape into an otherworldly snowscape. This

potential for the landscape to act not just as a terrain to be traversed, but also a

map in itself wherein the way forward could be determined by looking at the

ground under your feet, the hills on the horizon, or the stars above, seemed to me

evocative of a worldview in which wisdom and the natural environment were

bound together and inclusive of the more-than-human world.1

        I would later come to learn that these rock Cairns have a long heritage in

Iceland and were precisely used for navigation as I suspected. I would also come

to learn further facts that would help solidify my thesis: nearly fifty percent of

Icelanders believe in the existence of the huldufólk, the race of elves with whom

they share the island of Iceland; because of their belief in and respect for the

huldufólk, Icelanders have on occasion made policy decisions around

environmental issues by including the perspective of the huldufólk; and finally,

Iceland is consistently ranked as one of the most sustainable countries on Earth.2

Armed with this knowledge, some travel-induced inspiration, and a powerful

intuitive sense, I forged ahead. I had an intuition that cultural attitudes toward

magic were directly related to cultural attitudes toward environmental

sustainability.

       Divination is generally defined as “the action or practice of divining; the

foretelling of future events or discovery of what is hidden or obscure by

supernatural or magical means.”3 As someone who spent the last decade of my

life studying astrology, both as a practitioner and as a scholar, I have always

wondered, what’s the point? If the future is as yet undetermined, then why do

humans incessantly seek to know its outcomes by gazing at the stars, or reading a

deck of tarot cards, or a casting of sticks and stones? If the future is determined

such that we might be able to glimpse it, what good does this knowledge provide?

Is there any way to alter our fate?

       The only answer I have come to after years of research and experience is

this: divination, though a practice that appears to seek answers about the future, is

actually an act which looks to the totality of the present moment, in all its various

layers of manifestation and subtlety, to draw informed decisions based on a

dossier of information that is always available but rarely acknowledged or utilized

by normal consciousness. And, although it is a mysterious practice that we cannot

explain with current scientific epistemological structures, it is by no means

supernatural. Quite the opposite actually, divination is one of the most natural

ways that humans engage with the more-than-human world and, more

importantly, with the divine.

       I have personally practiced divination methods like astrology, tarot, runes,

and the I-Ching. I’ve researched from afar the African system of Ifá, Tibetan Mo,

Malagasy Sikidy, and Peruvian bone throwing. I’ve been amazed at the fact that

nearly every culture in the world has some indigenous form of divination and that

they all utilize basic materials from the natural environment. Despite all these

divinatory explorations it was not until that enlightening moment in Iceland that I

developed a particular interest in the northern latitudes. Although Iceland itself

has a rich tradition of magic, with prophecy and augury woven delicately into the

epic poetry of the Middle Ages, I would have to traverse the Atlantic Ocean to

Scandinavia to find a relatively complete divinatory system upon which I could

base my research.

       Seeing an image of the Sámi shaman drum for the first time was like

recalling a vision from a dream. Made of wood burls and reindeer skin, the Sámi

drum is in some ways similar to shaman drums around the world, especially of the

Eurasian shamanic traditions. But it is the top skin of the Sámi drum that is its

most distinguishing characteristic. Painted with an abundance of symbols that

depict features of the natural and cosmological worlds of the Sámi, this otherwise

ordinary drum is transformed into a book of mythic and ecological wisdom—a

‘“shaman’s bible.”4

       Being a significant feature of the mostly oral Sámi shamanic tradition, the

drum is used for everything from ritual and trance to hunting luck and prophecy,

but most notably, the Sámi drum is a rich and complex divination system—the

only such drum in the world. While most divination systems are uniquely married

to the natural environment in which they originate, the Sámi are the only culture

known to have embedded their divination system into a drum.

       The symbols of the Sámi drum lead deeply into the heart of Sámi myth,

history, and culture, but its ecological significance was the deciding factor that I

had found my research topic. However, the drum’s obscurity seemed

insurmountable for my research project and I did my best to avoid admitting that I

was in fact going to write a dissertation about a divinatory method that I believed

was no longer in practice, that had a sordid history of suffering and loss, that was

a huge part of the cultural heritage of an indigenous community with whom I had

no obvious ancestral ties and who happened to inhabit one of the most remote

(and cold!) corners of our globe.

       Fast-forward six months and I find myself stepping off a bus in Jokkmokk,

Sweden, a few miles north of the Arctic Circle to visit the Sámi Winter Market

and hopefully to “divine” a way forward with my dissertation. My desire to visit

Sápmi, rather than just to read the countless essays and books written by foreign

scholars, historians, and priests about the Sámi and their drums, was fueled by an

awareness of the subtle power plays at work when outsiders study and make

assumptions about indigenous cultures. I didn’t want to be an armchair

anthropologist. But I am also not really an anthropologist at all. I am a historian, a

philosopher, a student of world religions and mythology, and I am also a diviner.

       There is evidence of cultural continuity for the Sámi and their ancestors in

arctic and sub-arctic Scandinavia for as long as 10,000 years.5 However, as with

any culture, the Sámi people are diverse and unique: there is no single way to be

Sámi. What would conservative Sámi think of an American woman asking

the Sámi shaman drum. Not a relic indeed, but rather a symbol for cultural

                        Introduction: A Dark Enough Target

          The Arctic has enchanted the human imagination for thousands of years.

Etymologically derived from the Greek word άρκτος, the region was named after

the constellation Ursa Major—the great bear who eternally circles the north pole

star, thereby providing cosmic orientation in a world of extreme seasonal changes

and dramatic struggles between light and dark. These early associations with the

mythic realm have no doubt contributed to the enchanting qualities of the Arctic

region.

          In this tenuous era of climate change, much attention is paid to the polar

regions of the globe, and the Arctic is the primary stage upon which the epic

drama of our time is unfolding. The ecological devastation of the twentieth and

twenty-first centuries has been attributed to the industrial activity of humans, and

the starving polar bear—struggling to survive in a rapidly melting Arctic—has

become a compelling symbol for the tragic consequences of climate change. The

mythic άρκτος of the past has been replaced with, or perhaps manifested in, the

life and struggle of the very real twenty-first century polar bear.

          But there is more to the land around the North Pole than permafrost and

polar bears. The inhabitants of the circumpolar regions have survived in

constantly changing conditions for thousands of years and their inherited cultural

wisdom holds precious knowledge for humanity as we learn how to navigate the

uncertainty of the future by cultivating environmental sensitivity and conservation

in the present. As keepers of the sacred knowledge of the arctic landscape, the




                                           9
shamans, diviners, witches, noaidi, and priests of the north have much to teach the

inhabitants of lower latitudes.8

        During this time of environmental crisis and climate change, ecological

research has been dominated by the canons of modern scientific rationality. This

paradigm has focused on quantitative assessments such as rising temperatures,

glacial recession, sea-level rise, and the decline of species populations.

Furthermore, this paradigm has influenced the global discussion around

environmental sustainability, often prioritizing the future of human industrial

activity and consumption over nature’s inherent right to exist regardless of its

potential for commodification. The symbol systems of indigenous communities

can provide an alternate way of knowing both the challenges of and remedies for

the current climate crisis.

Communities in the North: Introducing the Sámi

nearly 8 million square miles above the 9000 miles-long Arctic Circle, the area is

richly inhabited by indigenous peoples, of which there are about forty distinct

ethnic groups with roots in the polar region.9 The Sámi people, whose territory

spans thousands of miles of the arctic and subarctic regions of Norway, Sweden,

Finland, and the Kola Peninsula of northwest Russia, are the only indigenous

Ancient Divination and Ecology

       Ecology and divination come together in the classical Greek world most

notably in the oracle at Delphi and the broader complex of oracular sites

throughout the ancient Mediterranean. Sarah Iles-Johnston, in a response to the

commonly applied distinction between natural and technical divination (i.e.,

divination that proceeds as a response to an unsolicited natural phenomenon

versus enquiries posed to diviners who seek the answer through applied

practices), suggests that another marked distinction of divination in the classical

world was divination performed at oracular complex sites versus that performed

by an individual mantis, or diviner.

       Iles-Johnston goes on to describe the sacred landscapes that supported,

both in myth and culture, the aura of divinity that surrounded the oracle

complexes of Delphi, Dodona, Claros, and Didyma. Delphi, located near the

Mediterranean Sea, high on the slopes of Mt. Parnassus, was formed around a

deep chasm in the earth. The Pythia—or priestess—sat upon a stool above the

chasm and “From the chasm pour forth intoxicating vapors, and as they fill her

body, she becomes possessed by Apollo. She speaks for the god in an incoherent

voice, and her gibbering message is translated by priests into poetic verse that

inquirers will be able to understand.”55 The priestess received her insights from

the god Apollo, but those messages were transmitted via the medium of a unique,

or atypical, terrestrial phenomenon.

       Dodona was situated in its location due to the presence of a sacred oak tree

that was said, in various mythic tales, to have been a bearer of prophetic

of stewardship or sustainable resource management, in accordance with the

criteria for religious ecology, the divination sites of Delphi, Claros, Dodona, and

Dydima are exemplar of earth-based phenomena that connect humans to the

cosmos, and teach humans how to live in right-alignment with the natural world.58

would not be too far fetched a possibility, I do not seek to distill all the world’s

spiritual practices into a single monomyth or—and perhaps especially—not to

state that these proclivities are resultant from genetics or neurophysiology. Rather,

the point of this discussion on the living-ness of the cosmos is to highlight the

results. Namely, when humans live amongst what Thomas Berry calls a

“communion of subjects” there is greater potential to develop an ecological self,

and thereby cultural commitments to sustainable thinking.70 Whether or not this

potential is inevitable or even likely is the ongoing question of this work.

Philosophical Orientation: Thinking with Drums

        It is nearly 400 years since French philosopher René Descartes famously

claimed “Cogito ergo sum.”90 As it is, humans are still busy thinking and being,

but what was for Descartes a simple truth has in the last century been threatened

by philosophical complexity around the concept of self. The modern self is likely

to proclaim “I think, therefore I am a” followed by a vast array of personal

attributes such as gender, religious orientation, or racial association. By focusing

on indefinite characteristics of consciousness and identity, the modern self is

consistently reorienting to new knowledge and assumptions about both self and

world. What was once the pinnacle of knowing—the human mind—has proven to

be more like a peak in a vast range of mountains that are cascading, crashing,

subducting, and eroding all at once and all the time. Thinking and knowing can no

longer be presumed.

Beyond Colonial Anthropology; Toward Indigenous Epistemology

       Structural anthropologist Claude Levi-Strauss posited that some

phenomena are “good to think with” in the 1960s, but today it seems the question

is whether some phenomena are better to think with. Is it better to think about the

psychic wellbeing of a glacier when assessing global ice health than to analyze

photographs from satellite images? Is it better to think of oneself as a reindeer

through shamanic trance in order to find a wild herd in the tundra than to use gas

and oil to power a snowmobile to traverse hundreds of miles across treacherous

arctic terrain? Is it better to think with something, rather than about something?

       Though difficult to parse out, the difference between thinking and thinking

with comes down to a matter of perspective. It challenges assumptions about

subjectivity and objectivity that were called into question by Kant but that still

dominate epistemological undertakings.92 By resisting the urge to distinguish

between subject and object, and by following a methodological process that can

The Role of the Indigenous in a Time of Environmental Peril

       As the world careens toward what is unanimously described as impending

climate catastrophe, it is critical that colonizing powers begin to learn from the

indigenous communities who have preserved vast natural territories for thousands

of years. This does not mean that the Sámi people of the Arctic or the native

peoples of the Amazon or the Andes are being called upon to ‘save the world’ and

fix the problems created by those who have already oppressed and violated their

communities enough. But rather, it is in the best interest of those who are indeed

responsible for fixing the mess to do so according to means that are tried and true.

An Overview of Shamanism in Culture and Theory

       Shamanism is not so much a complicated topic of study as it is a

misunderstood term that has been overextended in its use. The word shaman is

specifically referential to the central spiritual, healing figure of the Evenk-Tungus

tribe of central Siberia.109 Both colloquially and among academic circles,

shamanism has become associated with tribal figures that serve as the healers,

diviners, and wisdom tradition bearers of a specific community.

various tribes who are ‘shamanic’ in disposition, tribes that are often separated by

great distances in time and space, it is the universally recognized phrase. Scholars

Shamanism and the Modern Spirit

       Seeking a more shamanically inspired explanation, anthropologist Michael

Harner explains the similarities among disparate shamanic cultures as owing to

the efficacy of shamanic practice in the physical and spiritual healing of pre-

civilized cultures. He explains,

       These so-called primitive peoples lacked our advanced level of medical
       technology, so they had excellent reason to be motivated to develop the
       non-technological capacities of the human mind for health and healing.
       The basic uniformity of shamanic methods suggests that, through trial and
       error, people arrived at the same conclusions.115

Applying a practitioner’s approach to the problem, Harner’s solution requires us

to honor and believe in the efficaciousness of shamanic practices. Though

pragmatic in approach, Harner’s description requires a metaphysical foundation

that challenges the Western, colonial audience. Despite his obvious efforts to

break out of a scientific, rational mindset and his dedication to meeting

shamanism on its own terms—a method aligned with contemporary indigenous

those indigenous communities who still have living traditions.118

       The Sámi shaman emerges from history sometime between the last

glaciation and the early Stone Age.128 As the great glaciers that covered the

European continent receded, altering the landscape and the climate, humans from

the eastern steppes followed migratory species such as reindeer across the tundra

that appears to be directly related to the Sámi insofar as can be gleaned from

archeological evidence and symbol hermeneutics. The location of some of these

rock art sites and the continuity of their imagery suggests that the Sámi ancestors

inhabited a territory that extended further south than is generally assumed. One of

the primary features of these rock art symbols is a humanoid figure with a

triangular shaped head that also appears frequently on the historical Sámi

       The relationship between the powers of nature and the noaidi is complex.

The noaidi is both gifted his powers from the natural world and, with the

people, places, and more-than-human inhabitants of the Sápmi region, it is

       For the Sámi, the cosmos is alive and the natural environment permeated

all Sámi are aligned with the natural world in a way that matches the ancestral

tradition, there is consensus that the shamanic tradition provides and thrives on

the foundations of an ecologically sensitive culture.

       As discussed previously, the prehistoric origins of the Sámi noaidivuohta

were likely connected to hunting rituals and landscape features as depicted in rock

art in the Fennoscandic region. Juha Pentikäinen says that, “Shamanism appears

to have been the religion of the Sámi hunter-fisher culture; the central figure is the

spiritual leader, the “nojd,” who seeks knowledge of the supernatural world in

trances.”154 An important part of those ritual practices associated with the

prehistoric, and certainly historic, noaidivuohta was the use of divination, ritual,

and magic, to support the subsistence lifestyle of the Sámi and their ancestors. In

addition to the drum divination practices explored in this dissertation, “hunters

but especially in nature.

       Divination, specifically in the form of the Sámi drum practice, is a

guidebook to the spiritual and ecological world. As a practical dictionary of

mythology, the drum ensures that cosmic treaties made in the time before time are

honored as a daily habit of life. According to Helander, “Shamanism is a very

their rituals, activities, experiences and dreams.”156 Oracular use of the shaman’s

drum for both mundane events, like timing the start of a hunt, as well as for large

rituals such as a community sacrifice, brings the mythic cosmos into conversation

with the daily environment.

with the Sámi of that era.160 Thus, it seems reasonable to conclude that the drum

was an integral part of the pre-Christian Sámi tradition from early times.

The Politics of Ecology: Sámi Activism

       The polar regions of the earth are characterized by extremes. This is

intensified as a result of the current global ecological crisis and the people,

animals, and landscapes of the Arctic north are especially vulnerable to the man-

made conditions of global warming. The Sámi people have spent millennia

crafting a culture around the extreme features of the Arctic landscape and thus are

both physically and culturally at risk from the changing conditions. Guided by a

long historical tradition of nature-worship and living under the jurisdiction of the

most ecologically sustainable governments on the planet (not to mention one of

the most challenging natural landscapes), it is not surprising that the Sámi people

are inclined toward ecological activism. Indeed the twentieth century resurgence

of Sámi culture was catalyzed by environmental concerns and would come to be

characterized by calls for freedom of self-determination and regional authority

over resources and land-use.




                                          78
       During the years 1979–1981 the Sámi people were thrust to the forefront

of Norwegian popular consciousness when a handful of men, among them two

noaidi Eirik Myrhaug and Niilas Somby, protested the building of a dam on the

traditional Sámi shelter, in front of the parliament building in Oslo and began a

hunger strike. While other activist groups blockaded the dam building at the Alta

site in order to protect the biological diversity of the Alta river region, the small

group of Sámi in Oslo sought to secure political rights for the Sámi people to self-

governing their traditional territories.171 Although the dam was eventually built,

and the expected environmental degradation occurred, the Alta conflict provided

an international forum for the Sámi people to state their case and demands for

indigenous rights.172 During the two years of high profile negotiations and

protests, the Sámi people were seen as if for the first time by modern Europeans

and the global community.173

“Before Crosses”

          The story of the fate of the Sámi people during the sixteenth to eighteenth

centuries has thematic elements that can be found in many other places and eras

around the globe. Indigenous communities are frequently targeted by colonial

powers and on account of the resources afforded by their land—perhaps a

testament to their intrinsic stewardship and respectful inhabitance of a region—

are forcibly removed. The narratives that support these forced removals are based

on religious dogma or efforts toward ‘civilizing’ and education, but in plenty of

cases narrative is simply replaced with brutal genocide. In regards to the Sámi,

both religious and environmental concerns fueled the conflict.182

Poulsen’s drum has a unique aesthetic when compared to the other surviving

historical Sámi drums.216 Divided into five sections and depicting seventeen

bowl construction. The drum has one additional secret: attached to the four

‘corners’ of the drum’s underside are small leather pouches containing animal

bones.217 According to drum maker Fredrik Prost, who was invited by the

museum in Karasjok, Norway to examine the drum, the bones were identified as

fox ears in the top two pouches, and fox feet in the bottom two pouches.218

       Like the soft, rolling fells of the Fennoscandic interior, the Sámi drum

emerges from the quietude of history with a presence that invites reflection and

commands respect. The drum is a product of both myth and reality. Its

construction utilizes accessible, even superfluous, materials from the natural

environment, and yet it is shrouded in an aura of magic and mysticism. Each step

in the process of creation—the harvesting of the wood, the preparation of the

reindeer skin, the mixing of the paint—carries with it a wealth of mythic and

ecological knowledge. There is no part of the drum that is not ecological and yet

its function extends beyond the immediacy of landscape and resources. The

wholeness of the drum can only be truly grasped and appreciated in a broader

context, one that includes time and space, story and knowledge.

       In the way the drum is a reflection of the inner and outer world of the

Sámi shaman, the landscape of Sápmi is both a symbolic realm as well as a literal

place. This liminal quality is emphasized in modern times by the way in which the

Sámi themselves seem to inhabit two different worlds: that of their ancestral

lands, and that of the colonial states to which their land ‘belongs’. The drum as

decoration are in a direct and unique relationship to the natural world, the realm

of mythical and real reindeer, the trees of the circumpolar wilderness, the varied

terrain of the vast territory of Sápmi, and the other-than-human life forms who

inhabit the land. As a result, the drum is itself an individual ecosystem with its

own requirements for sustainability as well as a decided religious application. The

ecological symbols on Ander’s Poulsen's drum expose the variety of ways in

which the Sámi have engaged with the magic of the natural world.

       At first glance, Anders Poulsen’s drum may not look like the most

ecologically symbolic drum. When compared with the fishing and hunting motifs

of the southern style drums or the richly populated Kemi drums, all of which

seem to explode with nature-themed significance, Anders Poulsen’s drum gives a

very different initial impression.233 But his drum is no less reverent of the natural

world and it only takes a bit of symbol hermeneutics to see how Anders Poulsen’s

drum captures the extensive ecological commitments of the Sámi people, and,

perhaps, even reveals something about Poulsen’s own spiritual relationship with

the natural world.

       As previously described, Anders Poulsen was arrested on December 7th

to Willumsen, the court tribunal was especially interested in the playing of the

drum, and so, after a brief discussion of the drum symbols, they requested that

Poulsen provide a demonstration.235

Christian trinity, “And you God who hath created Heaven and Earth, the sun and

the moon, and the stars, all humans, and birds, and all the fishes and the sea.”236

the case for his defense. By invoking the spirits of nature as well as God the

Protestant church and colonial powers of Scandinavian kingdoms, the spiritual

externalized event. Poulsen’s invocation hints at this internalized syncretism, and

Weather Magic: Deities and Symbols of the Earthly Realm

       As early as the twelfth century, in the Icelandic eddas and the Historia

shamanism has proved useful for long-term stewardship of the natural world as

well as adaptability for the inhabitants of the volatile environment of the Arctic.

Reindeer in the Noaidivuohta

        A key feature of shamanism from all over the world is the unique

relationship between the shaman and the animal kingdom. The ability of the

shaman to assume the form of and communicate with the spirits of animals was

considered a crucial skill for maintaining balance between life on earth and the

gods.267 Through trance, drumming, or divination, the shaman could connect with

the spirit of animals and gain the sensory or spiritual attributes of that animal.

        For the Sámi, the reindeer was one of the most important spirit guides of

the noaidi. Bäckman relates how

        During the initial period of apprenticeship, i.e. during the transitional state,
        the candidate met and received the helping spirits which were to assist him
        in his tasks as noaidie. These spirits were modeled on animals—a bull
        reindeer, a bird, a fish or a serpent—which thus represented the upper,
        middle, and underworld strata of the cosmos.268

As one of the spirits that gives its life to the drum, the reindeer has a special

relationship with the drum. Reindeer activities were a primary focus of drum

divination. Divinatory queries often center around hunting, herding, and finding

favorable conditions for grazing and breeding. The reindeer was a significant

Sieidi as Ecological Memory

       Much in the way Anders Poulsen’s son, Christopher, contributed to the

ecological memory of north Norwegian weather history through his prophecy

about the stormy seas, sieidi sites preserve wisdom about the natural environment

about the sieidi construction, usage, and activity, can reveal information about

climate, ecological features, and changes in the natural world. In many cases,

overshadowed by the adventure I had on the first day.

       Eirik Myrhaug is an elder Sámi noaidi who is recognized as a leader

within the community. In his youth he was an engineer and he came into public

awareness during the Alta-Kautokeino river struggle when he was a member of a

and in the course of his career has trained many respected Sámi shamans. Eirik is

also a Sámi whose ancestors lived in the northern fjord areas where the festival

takes place and each year he hosts a pilgrimage of sorts to his family’s old sieidi

stone, Rikkgallo. I knew I had to go along.

between and among the realms of their cosmos. What might be manifest as the

beaivi in the earthly realm, ruler of the summer months and culminated in the

phenomenon of the midnight sun, is in another realm the aurora borealis, the

sweeping fox tail of myth and darkness. These correspondences extend

throughout the natural world. Myndash, the primordial reindeer whose mythic

origins secured the sacred relationship between the Sámi and the wild reindeer, is

reflected in the celestial reindeer bull of the heavenly realm as well as in the herds

Sun and Moon: Symbols of Timing and Orientation

       Cosmology encompasses more than just metaphysical worlds and indeed

speaks to the temporal experience of human life within a given worldview.

Temporality remains a key interest for divination, and as such is a worthy topic of

consideration. A large part of the divinatory process is looking toward outcomes.

When parsed for human endeavors this often means telling the future and as such

many divinatory systems contain within them methods for telling the timing of

events to come. There is no way to know whether Poulsen’s drum included this

particular component but there are a few indicators that suggest it is a possibility.

       In rows two and four there are two dominant symbols: a circle, and a

circle with a vertical median line. These symbols are said by Poulsen to be the

Sun and Moon, though there is some ambiguity regarding which is which.333 This

ambiguity combined with reports of shamans orienting the drum in different

directions for different purposes or at different times in the year (a practice which

is of course captured in the testimony itself) strongly suggests that the drum is

capable of being read according to not just seasonal but daily rhythms. The drum

is relied on for telling the best times to depart on a hunt, or to begin a sea journey,

The Ecology of Divination

        Like any landscape, the terrain of divination has natural features that are

shared and recognizable across cultures. In the service of initiating a process

wherein you might learn to see the world through the eyes of a diviner, I must

first point out these natural features and provide some context—cultural and

systemic—around their purpose and significance. The following is my theory on

the structure of divination systems. Ultimately, this field guide serves not to

replace your own journey, but rather provides a sensed awareness of how to

proceed once you take that first step.

       The structure of divination is based upon what I’m calling three

foundational pillars: symbols, layout, and interaction. The first pillar, symbol, can

be mythic, referring as it were to a cadre of mythic deities and stories that impart

understanding based on the connotations associated with a given symbol in the

knowledge about these objects to discern meaning and understanding of the

symbol. The Native American practice of animal totems and animal medicine

bodies as in palmistry, or internal organs as in haruspices. The designed spaces

also have a temporal quality to them and can either be static or changing, both in

their physical layout but also in their implied significations.

element can modify the interpretations of symbols inherently, or in specific

contextual situations, such as when a symbol might mean something if it is found

Poulsen’s drum, is especially unique in its symbols. Twenty-five independent

symbols are spread evenly across five levels. The symbols are mostly anthropic

images and there is a single reindeer and two circles representing the sun and the

moon. There are two buildings with crosses extending from the roof and a dozen

upright staffs standing alongside the humanoid figures. Additionally, the two

bottom levels have a number of figures drawn atop their own individualized

horizontal line, perhaps indicating sub-levels and mobility within those realms

described in Chapter 4.

symbol that it becomes applicable to the answer? Or perhaps the noaidi simply

way an actor animates words in a script, the diviner draws on a repertoire of

experience and intuition (and perhaps even a little flair) to summarize the message

multitude of meanings depending on temporal and spatial context.

       The Sámi drum is ecologically significant in that it can be read as a map of

both the inner world of the shaman as well as of the various layers of the so-called

material realm. As such, it can be superimposed onto any external situation. The

outer world, like the drum, is animated by time and interaction; where the

untrained eye sees a static landscape, or courtroom, there is in fact a stage upon

which any number of symbolic and meaningful occurrences can be performed.

The diviner as a trained witness interprets these messages not according to a set

rubric but rather to the infinitely overlapping qualities of a given moment.

       What could for Poulsen represent the direction of the nearest church were

he wandering in the deep tundra of Finnmark, may in a Norwegian courtroom

come to signify the very place in which he is standing. It is no wonder that

divination has been an undervalued topic of scholastic inquiry. Not only is it

largely un-dramatic (what novelty is there in a person staring at a pile of sticks

       Killing the protagonist is a challenging move for any storyteller to make,

and yet, in history it is all too often the case that our greatest heroes are made

martyrs. In the case of Anders Poulsen and his shaman drum, it would be easy to

focus on the court records, the artifact of his drum, the drama of it all, and

proceed to my own concluding remarks—to the end of this story—with nary a

pause to reflect on the feeling of it all. But ultimately, a man was murdered and

there is no research method or proven thesis that justifies this tragic outcome.

How then, does a researcher find the space to grieve?

        Part of the ongoing narrative of this work has been finding the courage to

diverge from traditional academic research customs without sacrificing the quest

for true understanding. One such custom that remains a steadfast tenet of

ethnographic study is the sharp distinction between self and other.407 Maintaining

clear boundaries between self as subject and other as object is one of the single

most important ways an ethnographic researcher can bolster the legitimacy of

their work. Impartiality is at the top of the ethnographer’s code of ethics, and

becoming too involved in the culture one is studying is to commit the greatest of

errors: ‘going native’.408

       But indigeneity is not a happy accident that befalls unsuspecting

researchers, but rather it is an inherited and cultivated way of life for people and

the land they inhabit alongside their ancestors and an extensive network of

think that individual researchers who spend years of their lives coming to know

researcher can mourn alongside the people they study with, not from a place of

pity, but rather as a witness.

Field Work as Self Work

        In his book The Wounded Researcher, Robert D. Romanyshyn suggests

that research is a process of psychological unfolding for the researcher, and that

by maintaining engaged awareness of soul, the researcher can both identify and

heal their own psychological trauma through the act of research. As a result of

this psychological approach to research, Romanyshyn describes how the process

can unfold in organic and unexpected ways:

        In working on this book, I am being worked on, and at times even being
        “worked over.” Thus, the content of this book is also its process. The
        Wounded Researcher is a direct expression of what it proposes: a work
        chooses one as much as one chooses it and there is always that gap
        between what one says and what haunts one as wanting to be said.409

As described above, research is not always conducted by the researcher. Rather,

the material itself is both pursued by and likewise lures the researcher toward its

core. What started for me as a vision to study the correspondences between

divination and ecology across the world ultimately revealed itself to be the

building of a deep relationship with a group of people via the life and death of a


        409
              Romanyshyn, Wounded Researcher, 16.

                                         179
single man. Rather than assuming the role of mastermind, I became a character in

my own research journey, and by acknowledging my own presence in the work

and in the world of those I’ve learned with, I have had the opportunity to connect

with the work heart to heart, soul to soul. It is as a soul then that I can find space

to grieve for the loss of this elder shaman, and it is as a soul that I invite you,

Reader, to mourn this loss as well.

        Romanyshyn describes how all research is a way of remembering, and as

such, is a way of acknowledging that which has been lost. He explains how this

aspect of research is, in a way, an act of grieving:

        Mourning, then, is not just the experience we have after loss. On the
        contrary, mourning is natural to soul. It is the way of soul, the soul’s way
        of knowing and being, the activity of soul that challenges the ego-mind to
        hold onto what it possesses by letting go of it. In a poetics of the research
        process, the ego is not the center of the work. The work is not in the
        researcher any more than a dream is within the dreamer.410

By allowing oneself—one’s soul—to be present in the research process, we are

invited to engage with our research topic with more parts of our self. This in turn

creates a space wherein the wholeness of the other is also welcome into the

learning space, creating a more authentic and personal understanding.

        This practice of relationship building is a key piece of indigenous research

methodology in order to avoid the pitfalls of colonial research practices. Echoing

Kuokkanen, Cram et al. explain that, “research and the contestation over what

counts as knowledge are just as implicated in the marginalization of Indigenous

peoples as are Christianity, disease, warfare, and constitutional maneuvering.”411

Divining Ecology

       There are few things that humans can rely on with any certainty. That the

sun will rise each day in the east, that babies will be born and children will grow,

and that death will fall upon us all; these are the boundaries of life on earth. They

are not negotiable. They are reliable. Outside of these boundaries there is, for all

intents and purposes, utter chaos. Within this chaos human life takes place and

partly a result of genetic signatures shared with our homo-habilus ancestors, we

humans have gone to great lengths to make tools that combat the oppressive chaos

of life. These tools have been both physical, such as fire, spears, and shelter, but

also cognitive, such as myths, language, and science.

       Divination is one of these cognitive tools and although arguments abound

over the accuracy and viability of the kinds of knowledge that can be ‘divined’,

divination has proven itself a worthwhile cultural phenomenon as evidenced by its

widespread practice. Divination speaks to and about the miracle of life; it invites

humans to actively and intelligently participate in the chaos of life. Divination

empowers humans with otherwise inaccessible knowledge that can aid in

decision-making and healing.




                                          184
        I set out on this journey with one goal: to show that favorable cultural

attitudes toward divination are specifically linked to the creation of a worldview

that prioritizes sustainable thinking. This is essentially the work of divining

ecology—to pursue a deeper understanding of the unseen subtleties that contribute

to sustainability practices within a given culture. But, like in many divinatory

acts, the realization of this goal also uncovered new understandings about

sustainability itself.

        In the context of this dissertation, which explores the Sámi noaidivuohta

and divinatory drum practices, I have uncovered a threefold understanding of this

connection. Firstly, in its manipulation and/or observation of natural materials and

phenomena, divination uses nature to look toward the future and therefore is

attuned with, and conscientious of, the long-term consequences of present action

within a specifically environmental context. The life of the reindeer whose skin

adorns the top of the drum and the age and location of the tree from which the

wood was carefully and ceremoniously harvested imbue the Sámi drum with the

essence of the immediate environment and traditional knowledge about its

stewardship.

        The details of these practices belong to the traditional wisdom bearers of

the Sámi people and are, as of yet, undocumented. It is my belief that through this

research I have shown enough parallels between the traditional knowledge

embedded in the shaman drum to inspire further studies and collaborations with

these wisdom bearers to uncover scientific data regarding the sustainability of




                                         185
these practices and the sustainable implications of the context in which they are

applied.

        Secondly, by relying on oral cultural heritage that is descriptive of and

rooted in various aspects of the natural world such as plant and animal species,

nature spirits, and landscape features, a divination system preserves fundamental

knowledge about the environment within the context of a cosmological narrative.

The Sámi drum exemplifies this feature in the complex symbol systems painted

atop the drum skin. The way these symbols are embedded in both geological and

cosmological landscapes provides contextualized, rather than compartmentalized,

knowledge, which is a known contributor to sustainability systems theory.

Additionally, as Kyrre Franck so elegantly expressed, the drum is a sustainable

symbol “because it is a circle.”418 Within the cyclical shape of the drum, and the

often spiraling layout of its symbols, the drum beckons its player to ponder the

cyclical and interconnected nature of life. Everything from the great deities of the

heavens to the berries eaten by foxes and bears is depicted in the drum. Nothing is

left out, and therefore nothing is forgotten. The drum encourages ‘seventh

generation’ thinking—a staple of sustainability practices among indigenous

peoples the world over.419

Looking Forward

       The role of the shaman evolves with time and culture. Historically, the

skillset of the noaidi tended toward the pragmatic, for example, in place of

telephones, the noaidi was skilled in clairvoyance. Lacking in advanced medical

systems, shamans utilized their skills for diagnosing ailments both physical and

spiritual. Where contemporary Sámi utilize the magic of GPS technology and

snowmobiles to herd reindeer, the noaidi of the past were sought out for their

skills at predicting weather, finding healthy pasture, and determining the best

hunting territories. Modernization has rendered many of the early noaidi duties

obsolete and along with this obsolescence, many cultural and ecological features

have suffered. Skills that were previously recognized as providing a particular

social or spiritual service are proving to have contributed to environmental

balance in more ways than expected, both in the region of Sápmi, as well as the

world over.




                                         188
        Understanding the full effects of this represents the horizon of my

research. It is here where the ethnographers and scientists must take over and

work alongside the tradition bearers of the world to both comprehend and analyze

the benefits of traditional ecological knowledge and the spiritually-based resource

management practices that have stewarded vast swaths of the natural world for

centuries. This work is well underway in parts of Turtle Island, Indonesia, and

even Sápmi, and has the potential to be utilized worldwide.421 It is my hope that

the theoretical foundations described in this dissertation will encourage

researchers to examine global divination systems in the context of their

environmental and cultural impact, rather than in the apparent efficacy or viability

of their predicted outcomes. Additionally, my hope is that the Sámi recognize the

inherent originality of their drum divination and are able to utilize it once again as

a traditional resource management technique.422

        It is not only the natural environment that has changed as a result of the

strained relationship between shamanism and ecological practice. Shamanism

itself has been altered in a number of ways. Primarily, there has been a shift in the

efficacy of shamanic skills that were once highly sought after. Changes in

physiological care of humans, both progressively in terms of medicine but also

regressively in terms of modern sedentary lifestyles characterized by unhealthy

                                     Epilogue

        Is there any way to right the wrongs of history? There are innumerable

humans who have suffered at the hands of religious (and other) authorities

throughout the ages. It is my hope—though certainly not my expectation—that

telling the stories of those who suffered on account of their beliefs will somehow

make up for the injustices of the past. Although an idealistic dream at best, and a

fool’s hope at worst, this has been the fuel that has supported my research during

the typical (and not so typical) ups and downs of the dissertation process.

        Anders Poulsen did not suffer alone. There are ninety other men and

women who were accused and punished for practicing witchcraft in Finnmark in

the seventeenth century. Their voices have been lost with the sands of time—their

stories never to be told.

        There are however people who have the courage and vision to raise

awareness of historical tragedies, and who work, very often through the arts, to

soothe collective pain and envision a path toward healing. In the case of those

victims of the witch trials in Finnmark, one artist has created a memorial for the

individual humans who exist beyond the statistics.

        The Steilneset Memorial is located on the small island of Vardø, Norway,

off the coast of the Varanger Peninsula, at the easternmost point of Europe. Built

in 2011 by architect Peter Zumthor and visual artist Louise Bourgeois, the

memorial is a tribute to the men and women of Finnmark who were tried for

witchcraft in the remote region during the seventeenth century. An austere

building, Steilneset Memorial is situated parallel to the island’s northern seaside,


                                        192
and thus subjected to the full onslaught of sea gales and coastal winds. The

interior of the long, narrow building echoes with the tumultuous sounds of an

inhospitable environment. Adorning the walls of the long hallway are placards

naming each of the victims of the seventeenth century witchcraft trials; their

gender, marital status, and accusations memorialized for posterity.

       The victims were mostly Norwegian women and roughly one-third were

ethnic Sámi, including some noaidi. The accusations (and violently forced

confessions) included dubious activities such as “selling wind,” “receiving powers

in a piece of bread and butter,” and, “playing the drum.” Walking down the long

hallway, which is essentially a gallows of time and history, one is struck by the

sheer number of victims, one after another, whose lives are revealed in brief

moments on banners, each hanging next to an eternally shining light. There were

so many. At the end of the hall, the last after the long walk of death, is the banner

dedicated to Anders Poulsen.

       Liv Helene Willumsen, the translator of Poulsen’s court records as well as

those of all the other victims, provided the content for the visitor’s guide and her

concise yet thorough descriptions of the circumstances surrounding each fatality

read like an attendance sheet, stating the bare facts without need for emotive

encouragement. The sheer number is fatiguing and makes the memorial one of the

more challenging historical museums I have visited.

       Louise Bourgeois’ specific contribution to the memorial is an installation

entitled “The Damned, the Possessed and the Beloved.” Located in an annex

building next to the main hallway, the installation consists of an array of mirrors,


                                         193
hanging suspended from high walls, all facing downward at the central feature of

the room: a burning hearth. In the center of the room sits a single wooden chair

atop which burns an eternal flame, a memory of the victims. To see oneself,

literally—through the mirrors—in the space of that room is at once shocking and

condemning. Am I the victim or am I the executioner?

       The Steilneset Memorial is but one of many artistic endeavors to

memorialize and vindicate the victims of the past. It is my hope that this

dissertation is seen not simply as a body of research, neatly packaged in academic

ideology and jargon, and set as an offering on the doors of the academy, in service

of new knowledge production. I hope that my research is also seen as an offering

to the more-than-human world, a service to the ancestors—to my ancestors. Their

stories may never be told, but their fate will not be forgotten.
